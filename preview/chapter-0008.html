Chapter 8: Hounds of Hell (1)

Until the age of fifteen, Baskerville children underwent two evaluations every year: a midterm and a final.

The family’s ordinary academic curriculum was already brutally strict, partly to shake off its reputation as a clan of berserkers. But the real reason for that reputation was the practical examination, which was so severe it bordered on murderous.

Not even children of eight or nine were exempt from it.

Baskerville children received a rigorous education from infancy.

From the moment they could walk on two feet, they were made to run endlessly across vast training grounds and steep mountain paths to build basic stamina.

They were not even allowed to lie down or crawl outside the designated times.

Guardian Knights served as instructors and drove them relentlessly.

During breaks they played with rubber knives, and when they slept, corpses of large demonic creatures or the young of smaller ones were placed beside them.

It was all to accustom them to the grotesque appearance of their prey and its savage ferocity.

At eight years old, their real training began.

Even their diet changed.

Tough, sinewy cuts of meat were simmered in broth made from chicken bones until everything softened into a thick mash.

The mixture was packed into sturdy intestines and boiled again until the last of the fat had been rendered out.

Eaten at that stage, it was the nourishing staple known as haggis.

For preservation, it was heavily salted and dried until hard, becoming the jerky Baskerville children always carried as emergency rations.

Today, the eight- and nine-year-old Baskerville children were sent into the mountains beyond the estate, each carrying a sack of that jerky.

Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain.

It was a harsh alpine region where only a handful of vicious demonic creatures could survive; its climate, terrain, and ecosystem were all equally unforgiving.

The young hounds of Baskerville were thrown into this place and expected to survive for roughly a month.

They were confined to a designated area. Along its boundary stood Guardian Knights known as Guide Hounds, keeping the danger within controlled limits.

The children abandoned in that unfamiliar environment were given two objectives.

Survive—and, if possible, hunt the largest and strongest demonic creatures they could.

Originally, survival alone had been enough. Hugo Le Baskerville, the current patriarch, had judged that too easy and added the second objective.

Some Baskerville children had simply hidden in safe caves or burrows and lived off their jerky until the month ended.

‘A child who fights demonic creatures head-on and survives should not receive the same evaluation as a coward who hides in a hole and does nothing but eat.’

That was Hugo’s reasoning.

Simply survive: 10 points.

Survive without suffering a serious injury: 30 points.

Outlast the other children and survive: 50 points.

Hunt a demonic creature and survive: 70 points.

Outlast the other children or hunt demonic creatures, and still survive without serious injury: 90 points.

There was no score of 100.

A swordsman died the moment he became complacent.

That was Hugo’s rule.

…And of course, death meant zero points under any circumstances.

Of all deaths, the most worthless was a dog’s death—a pointless death.

Eight-year-old Vikir arrived at Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain that day.

As the children climbed down from the wagons, reddish-brown mounds of earth stretched before them.

Black ants swarmed across the cracked ground.

A dry wind swept through old trees that looked half burned to death.

Everywhere he looked, there was only red and black.

The Guardian Knights began explaining the rules.

“Each of you has been given a badge. Once you leave this point, you may take one another’s badges. Taking another person’s badge earns bonus points.”

“Hunting demonic creatures also earns bonus points. Severe injuries cost points. Death costs all of them—though by then, your score will hardly matter.”

“Do not leave the restricted area. Move only within the zones we have secured over the last several days. Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain still contains many unexplored forbidden areas.”

The Guardian Knights went on to explain several smaller rules.

To Vikir, it was nothing but tedious nagging.

‘It really has been a while.’

He had visited Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain many times before.

In his previous life he had come here first as a young examinee and later, as an adult, as an instructor.

As an examinee, the place had seemed strange and frightening. Once he returned as an instructor, however…

‘It became even more frightening and unfamiliar.’

That was because instructors also had to search beyond the safe zone.

The area where Baskerville children took their survival test was, in truth, little more than a comfortable cradle.

The real danger lay beyond the boundary guarded by the Guardian Knight instructors.

The Forbidden Zone.

Beyond the ridge known as the ‘Cradle,’ and farther across several mountain ranges, powerful demonic creatures and barbarian tribes swarmed.

Ever since Baskerville claimed the region as its territory, the family had steadily pushed its frontier deeper beyond Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain.

The emperor’s orders and support were another reason for that expansion.

Every expansion of Baskerville territory was also an expansion of the Empire, and in return the family received tax privileges and broad autonomy over its military installations.

Before his regression, Vikir had spent many years here—as an examinee, an instructor, and eventually a hunting hound.

So the environment was deeply familiar to him.

That was especially true of the ‘Cradle’ inside the ridge.

…But most of the other children were seeing this environment for the first time, and fear showed plainly on their faces.

Cracked cliffs, the acrid wind blowing from dead forests, dry ash underfoot, and ominous gazes and cries from somewhere unseen.

“…Ugh. I’ve never been anywhere this awful before.”

“It’s even more barren than when I came last year.”

“You expect us to stay here for a whole month!?”

The eight-year-olds looked miserable.

The nine-year-olds tried to act indifferent, but their expressions were grim, perhaps because of what they remembered from the previous year.

In practice, the test was a struggle between the eight-year-olds trying to protect their badges and the nine-year-olds trying to take them.

For the eight-year-olds, who would return as nine-year-olds the following year, this first experience mattered enormously.

Amid the commotion, eight-year-old Vikir stood quietly and waited for the bell that would signal the start of the exam.

Nearby, several nine-year-olds watched Vikir’s badge with sinister smiles.

“That’s him? I heard he stayed submerged in the River Styx for seven minutes.”

“Seriously? I heard he strangled two venomous snakes while he was still in the Cradle.”

“And he got a perfect score on the written exam? Let’s see how good he is in the practical.”

Whispers buzzed all around him.

But there was one strange thing.

“…”

“…”

“…”

Highbro, Middlebro, and Lowbro—the strongest and fiercest boys in the nine-year-old class—were strangely silent.

The other nine-year-olds tilted their heads. The triplets were normally the first ones eager to teach Vikir a lesson.

Before long, a few children who had been watching stepped forward to provoke him.

“You arrogant little bastard. The moment the practical starts, I’ll smash your head in with a rock.”

“You’re nothing special, so stop acting so smug.”

“The next month is going to feel like hell for you.”

Only then did Vikir respond.

“I don’t think I’m anything special. Really, I’m not.”

The unexpected humility left the nine-year-olds looking puzzled.

But Vikir’s next words immediately twisted their faces into scowls.

“…It’s just that you’re all too pathetic.”

A barrage of childish curses immediately flew at him.

“You—just you wait!”

“Come to that old tree tonight, you little bastard!”

“Everybody move! I’ll deal with him! Don’t blame me if he dies!”

Baskerville children were quick to bite at even the cheapest provocation.

Like puppies barely weaned, they bared fangs that had not fully grown in.

To an old hound who had survived countless battles, their display was not even worth a smile. Vikir merely watched with indifference.

Just then—

Ding—!

Pavlov Van Baskerville, a Guardian Knight and instructor serving as a Guide Hound, rang the bell.

The practical examination had begun.
