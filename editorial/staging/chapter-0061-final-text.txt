The hunt ended in a feast.
That night, the Ballak set aside roughly one hundred kilograms of the finest oxbear meat.
They carried the offering away from the village and hung it high in a tall tree.
When Vikir looked puzzled, Aiyen explained.
“It is an offering to Madam Eight-Legs.”
Vikir gave a small nod.
He had already encountered Madam Eight-Legs once in the darkness of the jungle.
She was a horror far beyond anything an ordinary person could survive.
The Ballak apparently offered portions of major kills—and sometimes the bodies of condemned enemies—to her on high trees.
Strangely, no other creature approached the hanging meat.
Hyenas, birds of prey, and even insects kept their distance.
They seemed to know exactly who the offering belonged to.
‘Perhaps Baskerville and Morgue warriors captured in the past ended up as offerings too.’
Given that the Ballak themselves were not cannibals, the possibility seemed plausible.
◆◆◆
At dawn the next morning, Vikir woke early.
He slipped quietly out of his tent.
Several Ballak warriors lay asleep in the dew after drinking through the night.
Vikir stepped around them and headed toward the edge of the settlement.
His body was recovering faster every day.
To accelerate that recovery, he needed still more food.
The oxbear meat, blood, and organs helped, but Vikir decided to forage and hunt additional food for himself.
He had expected a slave to be forbidden from leaving the village alone, yet the entrance guards made no effort to stop him.
“Doing well in one hunt and now the slave thinks he can wander off by himself?”
Ahun happened to be on watch and scowled at him, but did not interfere.
“You killed one half-dead bear. Don’t start believing the jungle is afraid of you. Go get yourself eaten if you want.”
Ahun waved him away, and Vikir entered the jungle.
Before long he reached the tall tree where the Ballak had hung the previous night’s tribute.
“…!”
Vikir’s eyes narrowed.
The one hundred kilograms of meat were completely gone.
Nearby animals and insects had never touched it, which meant the intended recipient had indeed come during the night.
White dried slime coated the area, and patches of grass had been blackened and killed.
“…Nasty monster.”
Vikir left the site and made for a nearby stream.
The water was brown and turbid.
In places it was difficult to tell where river ended and swampy ground began.
Dense vines and thorny leaves were the only reliable boundary.
Vikir climbed onto a high branch and prepared a line.
He used one of the longest, strongest strands of oxbear fur as fishing cord.
The hook was carved from a piece of oxbear bone.
Vikir pricked a fingertip and let a few drops of blood fall into the water.
The response came at once.
Bubble, bubble, bubble…
Foam and ripples spread across the muddy surface.
Vikir hooked a nearby frog and cast it into the center of the disturbance.
Something struck immediately.
Snap!
A fish with saw-like teeth seized the bait.

Alone, the fish were cautious and timid; in a school, they attacked creatures many times their size.
They were extremely sensitive to blood, gathering in deep river pools and reducing prey to bone once a feeding frenzy began.
The frog bait vanished almost instantly, but the oxbear-fur line and bone hook held.
The fish had a dark body and reddish belly.
Vikir threaded the catch onto a sharpened branch.
For the next cast, he used a small piece of the piranha itself.
Nattereri reacted even more aggressively to the flesh of their own kind.
One, two, three, four—fish kept taking the hook.
Some specimens reached more than seventy centimeters and varied in body color, though the eyes remained red.
‘People say these are difficult to catch.’
Vikir looked down at the growing string of fish.
The species reacted most strongly to human blood and the flesh of its own kind.
Mana-rich human blood was especially effective bait.
The Ballak hunters apparently did not know this, so Vikir quietly continued building his food supply.
He roasted some of the fish over a low fire.
Some he ate immediately, some he smoked, and some he minced into fish cakes for later.
By the time he had also gathered freshwater crabs and turtles from the banks, the morning sun was fully up.
“…?”
Returning with food bundled in broad leaves, Vikir noticed something unusual.
A long line had formed in the center of the village before Chieftain Akwilla’s tent.
“What is that?”
Vikir scratched his head.
At least thirty people were waiting.
Every one of them was a young woman.
“Some kind of ceremony?”
The line stood directly on his path toward the central tents.
None of the women noticed Vikir approaching from behind as they chatted among themselves.
“I have been here since dawn. At least I am near the front.”
“I slept here overnight.”
“Damn. I should have come earlier.”
Vikir slowed, wondering what they were waiting for.
Their next remarks made the answer even stranger.
“After yesterday’s hunt, surely they will release him from slave status?”
“If he becomes a tribe member, they may arrange a partner for him.”
“I am going to make sure the chieftain hears my name first.”
“I watched him butcher the oxbear yesterday. He works like someone twice his age.”
“And he helped bring down an oxbear while barely healed.”
“He is not bad-looking either.”
Vikir began edging away.
“…?”
Instinct warned him before he fully understood the situation.
He was just about to turn around.
“What are all of you doing here?”
A sharp voice cut across the clearing.
The women in line recoiled almost as one.
Aiyen stood where all of them were staring, eyes narrowed dangerously.
Three freshly killed roe deer were slung over her shoulders.
She dropped the carcasses to the ground and addressed the crowd.
“I fed half of you yesterday, and now you are lining up at my mother’s tent trying to claim the hunter I brought back?”
One woman gathered enough courage to answer.
“If he is being released, you cannot keep him as your slave forever.”
“He is not even formally free yet!”
“You cannot monopolize every useful hunter.”
“If you want one so badly, go out with a noose and catch your own.”
“I have gone out plenty of times. I have never found anyone like him.”
“How far did you go?”
“To the Imperial border.”
“Then go farther next time!”
Aiyen snapped the answer and drew her hunting knife for emphasis.
The line immediately began dissolving.
Even bold Ballak hunters were wary of starting a real fight with their youngest hunting captain.
Arguments with Aiyen rarely stayed at the level of words.
Watching the others retreat, Aiyen snorted.
“Great. At this rate someone will claim him before he is even fully healed. I cannot trust that bunch to leave well enough alone.”
She turned away, still muttering to herself.
Then Aiyen and Vikir made eye contact.
Vikir had unconsciously paused behind a nearby tent.
He had done nothing wrong, but being caught listening still felt awkward.
Aiyen looked far more embarrassed than he did.
“…You heard that?”
“….”
“No. Of course you did not.”
“….”
“…Right.”
An uncomfortable silence followed.
Finally Aiyen scratched the back of her neck and walked over.
She was still noticeably taller than Vikir.
Standing close, she looked down at him.
The scent of sweet tree berries lingered on her breath.
“Mother called a council of elders last night.”
“….”
“Your name was one of the matters they discussed.”
Traditionally, an outsider who contributed the greatest prey to a major hunt could earn release from slave status.
This time, however, the elders seemed prepared to offer more than simple release.
“As the most honored hunter of yesterday’s kill, you will be allowed to ask my mother for a reward.”
Aiyen watched Vikir carefully.
“She may also suggest formally tying you to the tribe through a partner.”
Vikir was still technically an outsider and bonded slave.
From Ballak’s perspective, pairing a valuable outsider with an established member was one way to keep that person inside the community.
“…So that is why they were lined up before sunrise.”
Vikir sighed.
Aiyen narrowed her eyes.
“Do you already have someone in this tribe you would choose?”
Of course he did not.
His body was still broken, he was hiding in enemy territory, and his attention had been consumed by survival and recovery.
Vikir shook his head immediately, and Aiyen’s expression became complicated.
Relief mixed with irritation and uncertainty.
Thump.
Aiyen put an arm across Vikir’s shoulders.
Despite the force of the gesture, she was careful not to jar his injuries.
She leaned close enough to speak quietly.
“When Mother calls you in, I will tell you what you should ask for.”
Her eyes shone with a new idea.
“Now… what should I make you request?”
She looked as though she was already plotting something.
