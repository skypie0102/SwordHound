“This trade is void.”
In other words, the entire exchange was canceled.
The merchants flinched when Vikir addressed them in fluent Imperial.
A ragged youth from Ballak suddenly speaking their native language was the last thing they expected.
Worse, they had just been speaking freely because they assumed no one around them could understand.
“Y-you speak Imperial?”
“Did you hear what we were saying?”
“There must have been some misunderstanding.”
“Let us explain. Those words were only…”
The men who had been mocking the Ballak immediately averted their eyes.
They had suddenly realized that their insults and harassment were no longer private.
Vikir’s face remained calm.
“You mean insulting Ballak’s people and harassing a child?”
“Th-that was…”
The merchants faltered.
Vikir did not change expression.
“It no longer matters.”
They had been so shocked by his language that they had barely registered his actual declaration.
The trade was void. Everything exchanged that day was to be returned.
Fortunately, the merchants’ remaining goods had been gathered in one place, so the Ballak could hand them back without difficulty.
The merchants could likewise return the piled forest products to the villagers.
Aiyen understood Vikir’s intention and immediately ordered all trading stopped.
The merchants stared around in disbelief.
“What is the meaning of this?”
“Why cancel a trade both sides agreed to? We will lose money!”
“We do not come here every day. You cannot simply…”
The merchants protested all at once.
Vikir cut through the noise.
“Lose money? If this trade collapses, which side actually loses more?”
Aiyen looked at him in confusion.
She stepped closer and lowered her voice.
“Many people in Ballak wait for these merchants to come.”
She was right.
Some villagers were already crying or stamping their feet in frustration, while others stared longingly at the goods they had expected to buy.
Glass beads, potatoes, corn, harmonicas, and other rare imports had become objects of real desire because no one knew when another caravan would arrive.
Although Aiyen obeyed Vikir and stopped the market, the villagers looked deeply disappointed.
But—
“Caring about the villagers is good. First, look at the other side.”
Vikir continued his explanation.
“…?”
Aiyen followed his gaze.
“…!”
Her expression changed at once.
Ballak looked disappointed; the merchants looked terrified.
The merchants’ hands and legs were trembling from anxiety.
Vikir spoke simply.
“Most of what you brought is perishable.”
The caravan was loaded primarily with vegetables and grain.
Much of it would spoil or rot before the merchants could haul it back to the Empire.
Ballak, by contrast, had offered medicines, hides, spices, gems, and other goods that kept their value for a long time.
If the trade failed, Ballak lost little beyond convenience.
The merchants would have to carry home decaying cargo whose value dropped with every day of travel.
The merchants shuddered and tried another threat.
“If you treat us this way, we will never come back!”
“That is your choice. You are not the only people who would want access to this market.”
Vikir was not bluffing.
Once he eventually returned to Baskerville, he could use Cindywendy to establish a discreet and far fairer trade route with Ballak.
The merchants were trapped.
While they hesitated, Vikir made them an offer.
“I know the market value of both sides’ goods. Bargain honestly, and the trade can resume.”
Hope immediately returned to the merchants’ faces.
“How could a child possibly know frontier prices?”
“Imperial commercial law is complicated enough for adults.”
“Even if he knows something, there will still be plenty of margin left for us.”
The merchants laughed among themselves.
They decided they could still manipulate the numbers.
But—
“Four gold.”
“What?”
“Four gold.”
“No, that cannot be…”
“Four gold.”
Vikir knew exactly what the merchants’ stock was worth.
He also knew what Ballak’s rare goods could command once they reached the distant Imperial capital.
His confidence came from experience.
“I wrote much of Underdog City’s current commercial code.”
During his time as Deputy Magistrate, Vikir had been involved directly and indirectly in a vast number of trade and diplomatic disputes.
“Hellhound-tooth chess pieces can sell for around two million gold depending on the piece. Billiard balls carved from ox horns have recently sold near five million at auction. Naturally red ivory is worth many times that, and goblin chanterelles have become so valuable to medicine that ordinary price tables barely apply.”
The merchants went silent as Vikir recited local prices, wholesale prices, retail prices, and likely distribution margins for item after item.
Then he finished the calculation.
“After transportation, labor, risk, and taxes, this is a fair price for both sides.”
It was a real market valuation rather than the fantasy prices the merchants had imposed on Ballak.
Vikir selected only a small assortment of antlers, medicinal goods, and other valuables from the Ballak pile and placed them on the merchants’ cart.
“This is all your current cargo can honestly purchase.”
Only a tiny portion of Ballak’s original payment was needed.
The merchants flushed with anger, but they had no factual rebuttal.
Even under Vikir’s valuation, their trip would still turn a respectable profit.
It was simply a fair trade instead of a swindle.
For merchants who had expected an enormous windfall, that felt like losing a fortune.
They looked almost offended that the Ballak would receive anything close to proper value.
Then Vikir moved again.
He was not finished.
“Now we calculate damages.”
Vikir removed even the small amount of Ballak goods he had just placed on the cart.
Then he held out one hand toward the merchants.
“Pay.”
The merchants stared at him blankly.
To them, this finally looked like robbery.
Vikir, however, was still working from evidence.
“I am assessing punitive damages for adulterated food and the distribution of illegal narcotics.”
The merchants froze.
“What did we do wrong?”
“You truly do not know?”
Vikir pointed toward the grain and vegetables.
Among the produce were sacks of seed.
He reached into one and drew out a handful.
Tap, tap, tap.
Vikir poured the seeds onto the ground and selected several examples.
“You heated these just enough to kill their ability to germinate, didn’t you?”
Several merchants turned their faces away.
That was exactly what they had done: they did not want Ballak becoming agriculturally self-sufficient.
If the tribe learned to raise its own crops, the caravan would lose the dependency that made such exploitative trade possible.
When the Ballak realized the seed they had been sold could never sprout, anger spread through the crowd.
And there was more.
“You are also distributing cheap narcotics that are illegal inside the Empire.”
Vikir took several of the supposedly harmless glass beads and brooches and crushed them.
The beads had been advertised as charms that produced pleasant dreams.
Crack.
A fine white powder appeared inside.
It seeped slowly through microscopic holes in the glass.
Kept near a sleeper, the powder induced drowsiness and vivid dreams.
In reality, it was a low-grade narcotic capable of causing serious harm with prolonged exposure.
The same hidden drug had been incorporated into the beads, jewelry, perfumes, cosmetics, and similar gifts.
Vikir’s eyes hardened.
“Did Morgue or Baskerville knowingly allow this?”
It was not impossible.
A weakened Ballak population would be strategically convenient for the Empire.
But Vikir had no intention of sacrificing Ballak merely because doing so might benefit Baskerville.
For now, remaining trusted inside the tribe and learning Ballak archery was far more useful to his own plans.
He destroyed the contaminated trinkets and separated every heat-killed seed from the usable supplies.
The merchants’ faces grew paler by the moment.
Lord Smuggler and several others finally lost their tempers.
“Who are you to interfere? This is sacred trade, protected by the God of the Forest!”
“The Forest God has sanctioned our dealings for years!”
“Even Ballak’s own shaman has blessed the trade!”
They kept invoking Ballak religion because it had worked on the tribe before; Vikir, despite his appearance, was still an Imperial and was unmoved.
“These lands are legally administered by Baskerville, not by your interpretation of the Forest God.”
The merchants swallowed.
The statement was technically correct, but hearing it from someone dressed like a Ballak tribesman unnerved them.
Vikir continued in the same calm tone.
“If you insist on hiding fraud behind superstition, I can have the matter heard by the courts of Underdog City.”
That was not the sort of threat they expected from a supposed barbarian.
The merchants recovered enough to argue back.
“Our goods already passed Underdog’s food-safety inspection!”
“Under the previous administration. The new deputy rewrote the relevant laws, and several things you are selling plainly fail the current rules.”
“And who are you to tell us what the law says?”
The merchants shouted.
Vikir’s brows rose slightly.
“…You think I do not know the law?”
“Of course not! The new regulations are complicated and demanding. How could you possibly know them?”
Lord Smuggler threw the challenge back at him.
“What could a jungle barbarian know about the Vikir Special Laws?”
Vikir gave a single dry laugh.
He could not reveal his identity here, but the exchange at least proved his reforms were still being enforced.
“The trade is over. Consider yourselves fortunate Ballak is allowing you to leave alive.”
Aiyen delivered the final order.
She was furious to learn that the mysterious weakness spreading through the village came not from disease, heredity, or a curse, but from drugs deliberately introduced by outsiders.
Ballak warriors were already reaching for their bows, leaving the merchants no choice but to retreat.
By evening, Vikir had become the most talked-about person in the settlement.
The former slave had become the man who exposed the caravan’s fraud.
◆◆◆
When Vikir returned to his tent after the merchants fled—
Thud!
Something landed directly in front of him.
It was a smoked hind leg from a wild boar.
Curious, Vikir looked toward whoever had thrown it.
An unexpected figure stood nearby with his face flushed.
“…Ahem. Hm.”
It was Ahun.
He deliberately looked away toward the distant mountains.
“Well… thanks. For what you did.”
“…?”
Before Vikir could answer, Ahun raised his voice.
“How long are you going to stay half-broken? Get healthy already so I can beat you properly—or wrestle you!”
By the end of the sentence, Ahun’s ears had turned red.
“Eat all of it and recover quickly!”
With that, he spun around and left.
The large piece of boar remained behind as an awkward gift.
“What is wrong with him?”
Vikir knew some jungle mushrooms could cause powerful hallucinations.
For a moment he wondered whether Ahun had eaten one.
‘That would at least explain this personality change.’
Vikir was still considering it when—
“Hey, slave.”
Someone tapped his shoulder.
He turned to find Aiyen grinning broadly.
“I am not a slave anymore.”
“Wrong. You are my slave for life.”
Vikir frowned at her stubbornness.
Aiyen ignored the objection and continued.
“Ahun is different when his little sister is involved. Annoying as he is, he is a decent older brother.”
“…Sister?”
Vikir looked confused, so Aiyen explained.
“The girl you helped today is his younger sister. Her name is Aheul.”
After a moment, Vikir remembered her.
She was the early-teen girl who had joined the line when the village began discussing whether Vikir would be paired with someone after his release.
She was also the child the Bourgeois merchants had humiliated during the market.
Aiyen continued.
“Aheul has always been frail. Recently she kept getting weaker for no clear reason. Now I think those glass beads were responsible.”
That explanation fit.
Without Vikir’s intervention, Aheul would have continued accepting contaminated goods and her health would likely have deteriorated further.
Long-term exposure could have caused severe illness and lasting harm throughout the community.
In that sense, exposing the narcotics may have protected all of Ballak, not just one girl.
“Damn it. I let those merchants leave because killing cowards who refuse to fight is shameful for a warrior. Maybe I should have ignored that rule this once.”
Aiyen ground her teeth.
Vikir was about to answer when—
Rustle-rush!
A Ballak warrior came sprinting toward them.
“Message from the chieftain! All warriors are to assemble immediately!”
The urgency in his voice was obvious.
When Aiyen demanded an explanation, the messenger answered.
“The merchants we drove out during the day are plotting something. They are coming back tonight!”
Then—
Aiyen’s expression transformed.
Vikir had not seen her smile that brightly since entering Ballak.
