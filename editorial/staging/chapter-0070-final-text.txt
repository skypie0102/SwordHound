“Why is this person here…?”
Vikir could hardly believe what he was seeing.
He stared at the portrait for a long moment.
No matter how he looked at it, the young man was unmistakably Hugo.
Black hair, piercing eyes, a blade-straight nose, a strong jaw, and no mustache yet—there was no mistaking the face.
Why was Hugo’s portrait inside this pendant?
While Vikir stared, Pomeranian crept closer and tugged weakly on the pendant’s cord.
The small pull was clearly a request to have it back.
Vikir returned the pendant and asked carefully.
“Who are these people in the picture, and what are they to you?”
Pomeranian flinched at the stern tone.
Realizing he sounded as if he were interrogating a criminal, Vikir tried again more gently.
“Who are the people in the picture, and how are they related to you?”
The wording changed, but his tone barely did.
Pomeranian still looked nervous.
Then Aiyen stepped in.
“If you ask like that, of course she will be scared.”
“…? What did I do?”
“Smile.”
Vikir paused at the suggestion.
Then he produced what he thought was a smile.
“Who are the people in the picture?”
Pomeranian looked up and seemed slightly less afraid than before.
“Oh. It worked. You were right…”
Vikir glanced at Aiyen.
For a moment—
“…?”
Aiyen simply stared at him.
She seemed genuinely stunned by the expression before recovering.
“Slave… was that supposed to be a smile?”
“….”
Vikir immediately frowned again.
He had almost never smiled broadly in his life, so he could hardly blame her.
Then Pomeranian answered.
“Mama. Grandpa. Grandma.”
Vikir’s mouth fell open.
His thoughts accelerated.
A decades-old pendant, Hugo as a young man, an unknown woman, and a girl in her early teens—now identified as Pomeranian’s mother, grandfather, and grandmother.
“…What?”
A theory formed.
The young Hugo in the portrait was likely standing beside his first wife.
The girl between them would then be their first daughter.
What if that daughter had been taken into the depths and later gave birth to Pomeranian?
‘Come to think of it, I have heard that Hugo was not always as cold as he is now.’
Vikir knew little about Hugo’s life near the Imperial capital before Baskerville moved west.
But old Deacon Barrymore had once mentioned that Hugo had once been warm and devoted to his family.
At the time, Vikir had dismissed the story as absurd.
Now he reconsidered the history of House Baskerville.
The family had once been based much closer to the heart of the Empire before moving west toward Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain. That move had not been punishment or demotion.
Hugo himself had petitioned the Emperor to relocate the entire house to the frontier.
The reason was tied to the death of his first wife and the disappearance of his beloved first daughter among the tribes beyond the Black Mountains.
Afterward, Hugo’s hatred of the frontier peoples and monsters hardened dramatically, and he became distant even toward later wives and children.
Those losses helped turn him into the cold, calculating killer Vikir knew.
The Emperor judged Hugo’s abilities useful and entrusted Baskerville with expanding and securing the western frontier. Hugo buried himself in war and duty.
That was how the modern Iron-Blood Baskerville was forged.
Now one of Baskerville’s discarded hounds had found an unexpected relative in the depths.
Pomeranian. Pomeranian La Baskerville.
She was the daughter of Penelope, who in turn was the daughter of Roxana and Hugo.
If Vikir’s reasoning was correct, the small child in front of him was Hugo Le Baskerville’s direct granddaughter.
‘…That makes me her uncle.’
The thought felt strange.
Somewhere in the house, nephews were constantly being born, but girls were exceedingly rare.
Daughters were uncommon throughout Baskerville.
Hugo’s direct bloodline in particular was overwhelmingly male.
Even the wider family produced far more sons than daughters.
Baskerville always seemed full of boys.
“Hugo is exactly the sort of man who values sons and grandsons because he sees them as useful in battle.”
Vikir wondered how that war-obsessed patriarch would react to a tiny granddaughter he had never known existed.
He shook his head.
Every scenario he imagined was uncertain.
Pomeranian’s mixed frontier blood might make Hugo’s reaction even harder to predict.
Vikir placed a hand gently on the child’s head.
Pomeranian flinched, but she no longer recoiled from him.
Vikir lowered his voice.
“It seems Rokoko did not treat you well either.”
“….”
“Come with me.”
With Rokoko devastated and abandoned, Pomeranian could not remain alone in the ruined village.
But she clung stubbornly to the support post and refused to move.
Vikir followed her gaze and understood why.
A crude stone grave stood behind the barracks.
While Vikir considered what to do, Aiyen stepped forward.
She bowed once before the grave and spoke to Pomeranian.
“When our ancestors leave this world, they rise into the sky and become stars. Your mother will too.”
“….”
“What lies under these stones is only the shell she left behind. Her spirit is already watching you from the sky.”
“….”
“She would want you to leave this terrible place and keep living.”
Pomeranian finally raised her head; she seemed to understand Ballak speech as well as Imperial.
After thinking for a while, she loosened her grip on the post.
She took one step toward Vikir.
Black hair. Red eyes.
Standing together, Vikir and Pomeranian looked unmistakably related.
Pomeranian looked up at him.
“Mommy. Hair. Eyes. Same color.”
“Yeah. Baskerville hounds tend to look alike.”
Vikir lifted her into his arms.
It was the first meeting between uncle and niece.
◆◆◆
Afterward, Pomeranian was brought back to Ballak’s village.
“Uncle. Where are you going?”
She clung tightly to Vikir’s leg, already unwilling to let him out of sight.
“Uncle has a meeting. The chieftain called for me.”
“No. Uncle. I go too.”
Pomeranian was frightened of being left alone again.
Now that she had left her mother’s grave, the reality that she had no one else had finally settled in.
Her trust naturally fixed itself on Vikir, who shared her mother’s hair and eyes.
“…Fine.”
Vikir lifted Pomeranian and carried her on his back.
He searched for something that could serve as a carrying wrap and noticed a thick, coarse hide.
It was the oxbear pelt Akwilla had given him as a reward.
He had wondered what use he would find for it.
Now it made a sturdy sling for carrying Pomeranian.
With the child secured on his back, Vikir stepped outside.
“I may have to ask Chihuahua for help with her later.”
He could not leave Pomeranian permanently in Ballak.
Even if he did not deliver her directly to Hugo, she needed somewhere within reach of Imperial society where she could be raised safely.
That gave Vikir another reason to eventually leave the depths and return toward the cities.
“Maybe I should visit Morgue while I am out.”
He could also investigate how the Morgue–Baskerville alliance had developed during his disappearance.
Still considering those plans, Vikir arrived at Chieftain Akwilla’s barracks.
Inside, Akwilla, Aiyen, and the elders were already gathered with serious expressions.
Vikir and Aiyen were the principal witnesses.
Akwilla opened the discussion.
“You are saying there is a plague spreading?”
Both of them nodded.
They explained the destruction of Rokoko and the state of the abandoned settlement.
Other hunters added that they had seen dead orcs, lizardmen, and other jungle creatures with the same red spots.
Akwilla stroked her chin.
“The rainy season is nearly here. This is bad.”
Flooding would only worsen the danger if the disease reached Ballak.
Then—
“Chief, it is not a plague. It is a curse!”
Shaman Ahmen stepped forward.
He insisted that the Red Death was witchcraft and should be answered with sacrifice and ritual.
Several older elders agreed.
They called for a great rite, an answer that would conveniently restore much of the shaman’s lost authority.
Younger hunters argued the opposite: it was disease, not divine punishment, and Ballak should abandon the settlement before it spread.
The elders and younger generation fell into a heated argument between superstition and practical survival.
No decision came easily.
“Hmm. What should we do?”
Akwilla frowned.
Then someone silently raised a hand.
It was the person who had first recognized the name Red Death.
“I know how to stop this disease.”
Vikir spoke.
