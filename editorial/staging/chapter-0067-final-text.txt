The jungle had its own four seasons.
Summer. Brutal summer. Winter. Brutal winter.
Beyond the broad waterways and forests, those seasons had turned again and again.
Now another season was beginning over a quiet meadow.
Thud!
A dull impact sounded.
A Ballak warrior in his late teens recoiled and clutched his nose.
“My nose!”
Blood ran from Ahun’s nostrils as he groaned.
An impassive young man stood before him with one fist still extended.
He was tall now, with casually cropped black hair, cold eyes, and pale skin.
Vikir looked down at Ahun without changing expression.
“That is enough hand-to-hand practice.”
With the bout over, Vikir turned away.
Ahun accepted a companion’s hand and got back to his feet.
Watching Vikir leave, he spat a complaint after him.
“You bastard. You have become even more of a monster since you recovered.”
The warriors around Ahun snickered.
“You were always strong, but now your bow work is incredible too. People say you’re almost at Captain Aiyen’s level.”
“I still wouldn’t compare him to Captain Aiyen. And judging from that punch, he isn’t that terrifying.”
“Exactly. Ahun’s nose is bleeding, but any of us could take one hit.”
His companions laughed until Ahun spoke quietly.
“…It never hit me.”
“What?”
“His fist stopped before it touched my face.”
A chill ran through Ahun.
Vikir had indeed stopped his fist directly in front of Ahun’s nose.
The pressure wave alone had made Ahun bleed and rattled his bones.
“…That was only the wind from his punch?”
The others stared in disbelief.
By then Vikir was already a tiny figure in the distance.
◆◆◆
Vikir had turned seventeen that year.
His face had matured considerably.
His once-short frame had grown, and the roundness of childhood had left his cheeks.
Twenty-four months in Ballak territory had transformed him.
His injuries were completely healed, and both body and spirit had grown far stronger.
Once Ahun and the other young warriors stopped treating him as a helpless outsider, Vikir no longer needed to conceal quite as much of his strength.
Shk!
Beelzebub emerged through the artery at Vikir’s wrist.
The weapon had grown thicker and longer; he could now extend nearly a meter of blade.
Vikir tested it through a sequence of slashes and thrusts.
Crack! Krrrsh!
One boulder split apart while another was punched through by a clean thrust.
His swordsmanship had advanced enough that both cutting and piercing attacks carried frightening force.
As soon as the first two rocks fell, Vikir struck four more.
Six boulders were destroyed almost at once.
Torn, cracked, pierced, impaled, cut, and split.
They had been shattered by the fangs of Baskerville’s hunting hound.
“The Sixth Fang.”
Vikir had mastered Baskerville’s Sixth Fang.
The six manifested fangs withdrew as he let the heat and sweat drain from his body.
Mana now circulated through six stable cycles inside him.
His present level had reached the upper range of Sword Graduator.
‘But the wall before Sword Master still will not move.’
The Master realm was something else entirely.
Even after reaching Graduator again, Vikir remained far below what he ultimately needed.
Before regression, forty-year-old Vikir had reached the very peak of Graduator without ever crossing into Sword Master.
Between the peak of Graduator and Sword Master stood a genuine qualitative wall.
‘Upper Graduator.’
At that level, aura became so dense and viscous that liquid energy could behave almost like a solid.
Only by reaching and surpassing that state could Vikir fully recover his former combat power and begin challenging the Master barrier.
Even so, Vikir was confident he could already defeat ordinary Graduators in a real fight.
He could kill opponents above his nominal weight class.
That advantage came from Styx-hardened flesh and bone, Beelzebub, and the archery, tracking, and assassination methods he had learned among Ballak.
‘At full power, I can probably contend with the peak of Graduator.’
But he still disliked relying on a maximum-output gamble.
His true target remained Hugo Le Baskerville, a Sword Master, so far more growth was necessary.
With that assessment finished, Vikir returned toward the village.
At the entrance, young hunters preparing to leave waited for Shaman Ahmen’s blessing.
Dark charcoal still marked their faces.
The ritual was meant to keep the spirits of slain prey from remembering the hunters’ faces.
“….”
Ahmen stood silently and glared at Vikir, clearly displeased by his arrival.
Even so, he could not refuse to bless the hunter who consistently brought back the best results.
If Vikir succeeded without the blessing, it would only make Ahmen’s rites look meaningless.
So Ahmen smeared charcoal over Vikir’s face with conspicuous carelessness, leaving pale skin visible in several places.
“…May the gods of the hunt go with you.”
“…Thank you.”
Vikir needed no shamanic blessing, so the two separated without further argument.
Meanwhile—
Aiyen was receiving reports from hunters returning from the previous shift.
She listened with an unusually serious expression.
Vikir approached and asked her a question.
“Aren’t you hunting today?”
“Mm. Maybe later.”
It was unusual for Aiyen to turn down a hunt with Vikir.
Normally she would be the first to approach him, but today the report clearly mattered more.
“…?”
Vikir noticed the change but did not press her.
Carrying a worn longsword, bow, and arrows, he headed alone into the marsh without even taking a wolf.
The other hunters barely reacted; by now his simple equipment and modest appearance were known to hide results better than most full hunting parties.
Only one person kept watching him leave—Aiyen.
“Is he gone?”
Aiyen waited until Vikir had completely disappeared from sight.
The scout who had been reporting to her nodded.
“Shall I give the full report now?”
“Yes. Go ahead.”
Aiyen listened as the scout resumed.
“Four matters. First: the rainy season is approaching.”
Earlier, the search party had passed a stream and noticed an unusual animal.
It was a lungfish.
The species possessed lungs and could survive outside open water for long periods.
During the dry season it crawled through mud and buried itself deep underground, waking when rising humidity announced the return of rain.
Ballak did not normally hunt scaleless fish, which tradition regarded as unclean, but the lungfish’s appearance was a reliable seasonal sign.
Rain meant swollen rivers, disease, and other dangers, so the village needed to prepare.
“Second: we found suspicious outsiders.”
Aiyen’s eyes narrowed.
They were pale-skinned Imperials.
For a moment she considered whether they might be remnants of the merchant force destroyed two years earlier, but their behavior was different.
They entered quietly, left quietly, and did only one thing at the river’s source.
They carried a glass container filled with red liquid.
The strangers poured it into the headwaters and immediately withdrew through the jungle.
Ballak warriors captured one of them, but the prisoner swallowed hidden poison and died before questioning.
The only clue left on him was a dagger marked with the image of a single large snake.
Aiyen kept the dagger. She intended to show the symbol to Vikir later.
If it belonged to an Imperial faction, Vikir was the person most likely to recognize it.
“Third: an update on Rokoko.”
The scout continued.
Rokoko was Ballak’s rival tribe; where Ballak produced archers, Rokoko produced shamans.
The Empire would have classified many of their curses and rites as black magic.
Aiyen frowned.
Rokoko had been appearing in Ballak territory far less often than usual.
That was strange because Rokoko outnumbered Ballak by almost ten to one.
Then came the last report.
“Fourth: another Morgue search party.”
That final item drew Aiyen’s full attention.
“Again?”
“Yes. They are coming more often.”
“Same commander?”
“Yes. Her again.”
Aiyen’s expression tightened.
For the past two years, Morgue search teams had repeatedly combed the frontier.
Their commander had never changed.
Camus Morgue.
She was getting close.
