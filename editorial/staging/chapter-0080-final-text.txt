When Vikir first entered the ward reserved for the most seriously ill patients, the priests reacted immediately.
“This is no place for an outsider.”
“You could be infected. Leave now.”
“We do not need help from someone who will only get in the way.”
Their attitude changed after Vikir began moving among the patients and demonstrating what he knew.
“You are wasting holy power that way.”
Vikir corrected a priest focusing everything on one visible red spot.
In Quovadis doctrine, priests prayed to a god for a share of divine power and used that power to reproduce fragments of heavenly miracles on earth.
At the simplest level, the priest asked, the god granted power, and the priest used it to heal.
If the patient shared the same faith, that relationship could amplify the effect.
More holy power could be drawn for someone connected to the same divine system.
The effect became stronger when priest or patient had accumulated devotion through prayer, service, or evangelism.
Vikir privately compared the system to credit and lending.
But—
Priests normally believed divine power had a fixed personal limit.
That limit reflected accumulated devotion.
Prayer, self-restraint, sacrifice, charity, service, donations, and years of religious life all contributed to how much holy power a priest could normally call.
Someone with a small reserve could perform only a small number of miracles; someone with a larger reserve could perform more.
That was accepted common sense.
But—
Vikir knew another method.
He had seen priests in the Age of Destruction perform far beyond what their normal reserves should have allowed.
“…There was a method they called shorting.”
The desperate technique had emerged when future priests faced more wounded people than their available holy power could possibly treat.
The name came from the commercial idea of selling something before actually possessing it.
In finance, the seller borrowed what was needed now and repaid the obligation later.
During the Age of Destruction, priests faced endless waves of demons and even larger numbers of wounded people.
Ordinary reserves of holy power could not match the catastrophe.
The gods associated with Rune worship responded to the crisis.
Priests with long records of faith and service were allowed to draw far more power than before.
That let experienced clergy produce miracles on an emergency scale.
Even that eventually proved insufficient.
Demons from the other world devastated human society faster than healers could respond.
The religious system was pushed to an extreme.
Then some priests discovered they could draw on power before they had fully earned or restored it.
They performed miracles first and accepted the spiritual cost afterward.
Even when their normal reserves were empty, some future priests found that holy power could still answer them.
The principle became: save the person now, settle the debt later.
Future devotion, service, accumulated merit, and restored reserves could repay what had been drawn in advance.
The process resembled borrowing holy power against future religious merit.
Some called it borrowing; others called it shorting; the most self-deprecating priests called it begging from the gods.
During the Demon War, even non-priests who accumulated enormous merit by killing demons sometimes gained limited access to holy power.
Vikir had slain countless demons in his first life, leaving deep traces of that karma on his soul.
Some of that accumulated spiritual qualification remained meaningful even after regression.
As a result, Vikir could access a small amount of holy power despite not being Quovadis clergy.
There was one major restriction: outside Rune faith, he could do so only inside a sufficiently powerful sanctuary.
House Quovadis territory was exactly such a sanctuary.
Vikir approached one of the moaning patients and reached out.
Flash.
A thin stream of holy power flowed from him and eased the patient’s condition.
The priests stared and hurried closer.
None of them had ever seen a non-Quovadis outsider casually use holy power.
More surprising was how efficiently Vikir handled what little power he could draw.
“I learned by surviving the Age of Destruction.”
In his previous life, Vikir had watched desperate battlefield priests work over countless wounded people. He had learned their methods through observation.
The central principle was efficiency.
Rather than wasting power broadly, future healers learned to focus it as close as possible to the actual source of damage.
Traditional distant blessings and broad contact healing often dispersed more power than the battlefield could afford.
Concentrating the effect at the injured area reduced that waste.
Vikir adapted that principle while treating the Red Death patients.
Quovadis territory was saturated with grace, giving him more holy power to draw on than he could use elsewhere.
Even so, he could not sustain the effect for long because he was not an ordained priest.
Priests who saw the masked stranger’s results gradually gathered around him.
Methods that had become basic survival knowledge in the future were unheard-of innovations in the present.
And there was more.
The priests had not yet seen everything Vikir knew.
Vikir also used combinations of frontier herbs to slow patients’ deterioration and reduce suffering.
He had learned much of that practical plant knowledge during his two years with Ballak.
The mixtures were not a cure for the Red Death.
They were only meant to buy time and reduce fever and pain until real healing could be applied.
The recipes used ingredients and traditions largely unknown inside the Empire. Even limited relief was enough to make the ward quieter and more manageable.
Vikir continued moving through the patients, accepting filth, anger, and desperate complaints without reacting.
He had several reasons for doing so.
First, he wanted Quovadis priests confident enough to confront the outbreak; second, he needed concentrated holy healing for Aheul; third, he felt responsibility toward people endangered by the outbreak he had deliberately triggered here.
“….”
The priests watched him work in stunned silence.
Then—
“Mommy! Mommy!”
A child cried while shaking an unconscious woman.
The woman was pale, with only a few red spots visible so far.
Even at that early stage, her body was dangerously weak.
Vikir placed one hand on the child’s head.
“Do not worry. We will treat your mother.”
He helped remove the Red Death from the woman with the holy power available in the sanctuary.
Then he took several gold coins from his pocket and handed them to the child.
“The illness is only part of the problem. Buy food and firewood so your mother can recover.”
The crying child bowed repeatedly.
Other poor patients and families saw the money and gathered around Vikir.
They stretched out their hands as well.
One priest warned him.
“If you start giving material aid personally, there is no natural stopping point. You will empty your entire purse.”
Vikir shrugged.
“Of course there is a limit. The limit is whatever is in my purse. I can give all of that.”
He distributed the remaining money among sick families.
When the leather pouch was empty, he discarded it without regret and returned to work.
The priests were stunned all over again.
Dolores was among them.
“….”
She watched Vikir’s back and remembered the men who had followed her earlier.
Those young nobles had advertised their status and wealth while bringing conspicuous carts of donations.
Yet their charity had been mixed with arrogance and contempt for the people receiving it.
Vikir, by contrast, had simply emptied his own pocket without hesitation.
The amount was smaller, but the sacrifice was personal.
Dolores remembered scriptural teaching that a poor person’s bowl of porridge could mean more than a rich person’s thousand gold coins.
She watched in silence.
In the dirtiest place in St. Mecca, the masked stranger worked harder than anyone else for people who could give him nothing.
He carried himself like someone accustomed to the front line of a terrible war.
Again Dolores saw a pilgrim, seeker, or prophet in him.
His powerful body seemed mismatched with the lonely sorrow she sensed in his soul.
He called himself a hound, yet to her he still felt like an exhausted, lost animal.
It was as if his soul itself were bleeding.
‘Where did this man come from?’
She remembered the theological line he had quoted earlier.
“Theology is ultimately the study of understanding human beings.”
Then—
Thump.
Dolores felt her pulse jump.
Her heartbeat suddenly seemed much louder than the ward around her.
For all her saintly gifts, she was still a sixteen-year-old girl.
Dolores’s heart began beating faster.
And the feeling was directed toward the faceless man working in front of her.
