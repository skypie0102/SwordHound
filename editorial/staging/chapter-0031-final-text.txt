The story of the flower girl who received ten billion gold for pulling out a wooden stake spread rapidly through Underdog City and across Baskerville territory.
Underdog’s administration even assigned guards to protect Judy and her mother from people who might target the fortune.
‘This is how the law works. It will be upheld.’
The newly appointed Deputy Magistrate’s short declaration was repeated everywhere.
Naturally, the so-called Vikir Special Law soon became more famous among ordinary people than Baskerville’s old territorial statutes.
Gossips discussed it endlessly.
“The new Deputy Magistrate really is different.”
“They say once Vikir makes a promise, he follows through no matter who is involved.”
“They say crime in Underdog has already fallen by half since that day.”
“People used to say the law was far away and the fist was close. Now the law feels close enough to push the fist away.”
Break the law and face punishment; obey the law and receive what the law promises.
That simple principle struck the citizens more deeply than any speech.
A society that actually honored its own rules.
Much of the old distrust created by selective enforcement began to wash away.
From the day after the special law was proclaimed, open illegal activity visibly declined and the crime rate fell by roughly half.
One public demonstration had cleared away a large portion of the darkness that had long covered Underdog.

Underdog City Hall.
Chief Secretary Chihuahua Baskerville smiled as he wrote characters onto a plaque.

The plaque would hang in the Deputy Magistrate’s office from that day forward.
Vikir silently watched Chihuahua’s brushwork, then spoke.
“Your handwriting is excellent, Chihuahua.”
“Of course. I’ve never met anyone with better penmanship than me. Previous Magistrates made me sign for them so often that I even became good at imitating other people’s handwriting…”
Chihuahua stopped mid-sentence.
Vikir had said something completely unexpected.
“Could you teach me that too?”
Chihuahua’s eyes widened.
It was the first time someone from Baskerville’s ruling bloodline had asked him for something as a request rather than issuing an order.
More surprising still, a swordsman was showing genuine interest in brushwork.
“Of course! I’d be honored to teach you.”
“Thank you. I’ll ask when I have time.”
Pleased by the praise, Chihuahua hummed as he worked the brush.
Vikir watched him thoughtfully.
Chihuahua Baskerville.
He had been born a commoner, served Baskerville for three years after entering from outside, and eventually earned the family surname.
Unlike swordsmen who could become Guardian Knights and receive Baskerville sword instruction, administrative retainers received lower pay and no comparable secret training.
Warriors had an obvious reason to seek formal adoption into Baskerville—the family sword arts could raise their strength dramatically—but administrators like Chihuahua gained little beyond status and the chance to serve.
‘If I remember correctly, he joined Baskerville because he genuinely wanted to serve his hometown.’
Vikir had encountered Chihuahua several times in his first life.
Despite his sly appearance, Chihuahua was fundamentally upright and had a habit of speaking frankly even to Magistrates and Deputy Magistrates.
That honesty pushed him to the margins, and he died old without ever receiving a position worthy of his talent.
Vikir considered that history in silence.
“By the way, Deputy Magistrate, what you did was remarkable.”
Chihuahua finished the plaque and turned toward Vikir.
“Who would have imagined a performance like that? I’ve worked here for twenty years, and I’ve never seen the city respond so strongly. I’m still amazed.”
Vikir gave a small nod.
“Baskerville’s laws are actually well constructed. But writing good laws is not enough. What matters is whether people believe those laws will truly be followed.”
“Exactly. Unfortunately, the previous Deputy Magistrates never understood that.”
“There was no point getting angry at citizens for distrusting laws they had watched officials ignore. I needed something that would seize their attention and leave a permanent impression.”
The idea itself was something Vikir remembered from a ruler of the future.
Camus Morgue—the woman later known as the Heavenly Empress—would use similarly dramatic methods after taking control of her own territory.
Under her terrifyingly strict rule, people became so disciplined that lost money could remain untouched on the road and public quarrels nearly disappeared.
‘…Though her reign of fear certainly did not make her beloved.’
Even so, during the war against demons, Camus’s iron-fisted government dramatically increased human survival; after the Age of Destruction, Morgue territory retained one of the largest surviving populations.
That future history was not Vikir’s immediate concern.
“Now we deal with what comes next.”
Vikir looked down at a map of Underdog City.
“After paying ten billion in prize money, we should refill the treasury.”
He picked up a pen and began drawing red X marks across the map.
Vikir marked narrow streets, alleys, warehouses, and hidden corners as though he already knew every one of them.
X. X. X. X. X…
In moments, more than a hundred locations had been marked.
“These are places where illegal organizations are hiding now or will hide in the future. We’ll raid them one by one and return their illicit money to the treasury.”
“H-how do you know criminal organizations operate in all these places?”
“I can smell them.”
Vikir touched his nose with a faint smile.
Baskerville hounds were famous for their sense of smell.
Leaving the bewildered Chihuahua to interpret that answer however he wished, Vikir continued outlining the plan.
“Military doctrine says the best supplies are taken from the enemy. Seizing the assets of these organizations will restore tax revenue, and we can use part of it to strengthen relief for the poor.”
Chihuahua asked whether so many organizations could still remain after the crime rate had fallen by half; Vikir answered that the public spectacle had suppressed overt crime, while the groups that remained were simply becoming quieter and more sophisticated.
That did not matter.
Vikir knew the locations of these organizations and the identities of the people at their centers.
‘I patrolled and raided places like these endlessly in my first life.’
He still vividly remembered hunting criminals through dark alleys during the old war on crime.
Some of his former comrades had accepted bribes, which had also taught him the methods used to corrupt enforcement from inside.
He knew how criminals thought, where they hid, what businesses they used, how their networks behaved, and how far they were willing to go.
He even remembered the channels through which underground money would move for roughly the next twenty-five years.
Buzz… buzz…
Beelzebub, hidden in the artery of Vikir’s wrist, twitched faintly as his killing intent sharpened.
The demonic artifact hungered for blood.
Just then—
“Deputy Magistrate!”
The door opened and a clerk entered with a troubled expression.
“…Someone is requesting an audience.”
Vikir and Chihuahua both turned toward him.
Chihuahua spoke first.
“Who requests an official audience when the Deputy Magistrate has not even been in office for two full days?”
“The City Hall Youth Self-Governance Committee would like to meet him…”
Chihuahua’s face immediately hardened.
So they had finally come.
Vikir broke the silence.
“In other words, the locally important young heirs want to see me?”
“…Yes. The committee is made up mostly of second- and third-generation members of influential local families.”
They were heirs of smaller houses that had exercised power inside Baskerville territory for generations.
Most had direct or indirect ties to Baskerville through marriage, blood, schooling, business, or old patronage.
Chihuahua could barely hide the contempt in his voice.
“They call themselves the City Hall Youth Self-Governance Committee, but in truth they’re a self-satisfied social club. Apparently they have something to say about the Vikir Special Law.”
“…Hmm.”
Vikir leaned back and rested his chin on one hand.
‘When you make a law to root out corruption, the most corrupt places complain the loudest.’
The first organized backlash against the Vikir Special Law had arrived through the committee.
Turning to Chihuahua, who was watching him anxiously, Vikir gave a short answer.
“The flies smelled it first.”
That was why he had laid out bait from his first day in office.
Now it was time to remove the pests feeding on the city.
