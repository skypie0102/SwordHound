Seven years earlier.
House Morgue had an exceptionally headstrong eight-year-old girl.
Camus Morgue.
She was the youngest of the family’s leading candidates for its future leadership.
Her extraordinary talent had been obvious from the beginning.
One day, the children of House Morgue gathered for a lesson in magic.
“The path of magic is dangerous. When you stare into the abyss of magic, the abyss stares back. Venture too deeply and something greater than you may devour you, so remain cautious.”
The tutor’s warning filled most of the children with vague fear.
Only Camus scoffed.
“Nonsense.”
The tutor frowned.
“Lady Camus, why do you call it nonsense?”
“You said that when I stare into the abyss, the abyss stares into me. That makes no sense.”
Camus looked at the tutor without expression.
“The abyss of magic is so vast and deep that the human mind cannot comprehend it.”
“….”
“If an ant sees me, that does not mean I notice the ant. The abyss may not even know I am looking at it, much less care. Fearing that it will stare back is rather self-important.”
“….”
“Perhaps matters change after reaching a certain level. But there is no reason to frighten children who cannot even control their mana yet.”
The tutor fell silent.
Camus’s argument was difficult to dismiss.
Even after reaching the Fifth Circle and forming five mana circles, the tutor had never personally been consumed by any mystical abyss.
Perhaps things would be different near the Seventh Circle, regarded as the extreme limit of ordinary magic.
When the tedious lesson ended, Camus rose and left.
‘What is Uncle doing?’
Adolf was the one person in the family who understood her talent and with whom she felt comfortable speaking openly.
Camus was closer to her uncle Adolf Morgue than even to her mother, Raspane Morgue, the family head.
Then,
Camus overheard Raspane and Adolf speaking in the family head’s room.
“You are saying the ruby vein continues beneath Baskerville territory?”
“Yes, Sister. Some kind of confrontation seems unavoidable.”
“Troublesome. I have no desire to bow to that stubborn Hugo. And barbarians are active there as well. Are we looking at a three-way conflict?”
“The vein extends into Baskerville land, so legally we are at a disadvantage. The barbarians there have also troubled Baskerville for years. A local war would cost us heavily in several ways.”
Raspane and Adolf looked grave.
Camus stepped forward.
“Let me handle it.”
Raspane and Adolf stared at her in surprise.
“Does this child even understand how serious this is…?”
“Wait, Sister. Hear Camus out. She is a genius. She may see something the adults have overlooked.”
That was exactly why Camus liked her uncle Adolf.
Raspane and Adolf listened, skeptical at first.
“Hmm. So we use the fact that you are a child to open the conversation?”
“Then we strengthen cooperation and push for joint development afterward? Hmm. That is not a bad idea.”
The plan was difficult to believe had come from an eight-year-old.
Then,
“Why not discuss a marriage alliance at the same time? Working together as relatives would be better than either side simply backing down.”
It was, in effect, a marriage-alliance strategy. Camus had proposed another political solution.
Raspane’s eyes brightened.
“A marriage pact with Baskerville? Then whom should we offer?”
“What about Highsis, Midsis, and Lowsis? They are not especially useful for anything else.”
Camus answered casually.
House Morgue immediately began negotiations.
Adolf brought Camus with him to House Baskerville, where her age let her speak with Hugo in a way an adult envoy could not.
She explained the underground vein with simple physical analogies that made the territorial problem easy to understand.
But for the first time in her life, Camus suffered a clear defeat.
‘That boy.’
An eight-year-old child stepped forward from the Baskerville side.
Camus did not fully understand his reasoning at first, but she knew she had lost the exchange.
The frustration was so intense that she nearly cried.
She had never experienced anything like it.
No child her age, and hardly any teenager, had ever outdone her.
Even adults rarely caught her off guard.
The world had always moved more or less as Camus expected.
But that day was different.
Everything slipped outside her control.
And the reason was the dark-haired boy she had met that day—‘that boy.’
‘If it were not for you…!’
Camus became furious and wanted to defeat the first person who had ever truly frustrated her.
So she challenged him in the training grounds, where sword and magic met.
She attacked with everything she had.
But the boy kept evading her.
Thump! Thump, thump!
Worse, he kept flicking her forehead as though the entire duel were a game.
Being struck in the same place over and over genuinely hurt.
His irritating face seemed to fill her vision.
Anger narrowed her world until it felt as though only the two of them existed.
All she could think about was landing one clean hit.
Then,
Boom!
An accident erupted in the adjacent training area.
A violent blast tore through the grounds and ruined Camus’s clothing.
She hid behind the cloud of dust and smoke, but knew it would clear within seconds.
Only then did embarrassment overwhelm her.
There were many people nearby besides Camus and the boy.
For the proud young genius of House Morgue, the prospect of being exposed before everyone was unbearable.
She had spent her life being admired as Morgue’s prodigy and future leader; now all that pride felt as though it might collapse in a moment.
Even an eight-year-old had dignity.
Camus fought desperately not to cry.
She could not bear the thought of being humiliated in front of the same children she had always looked down on.
Especially not with Highsis, Midsis, and Lowsis training nearby.
If they saw her crying after the accident, they would tease her for the rest of her life.
‘…What am I supposed to do?’
There seemed to be no escape.
Soon the dust would settle and everyone would see what had happened.
‘I would rather die.’
For one desperate instant, Camus truly thought so.
Then,
Flutter!
Her view suddenly went dark.
Something heavy, warm, and protective settled around her.
When Camus looked up, the boy was standing directly in front of her.
He had taken off his Baskerville cloak and wrapped it around her so she would not be exposed.
‘Isn’t he embarrassed at all?’
Camus wondered, but the boy seemed completely unconcerned with the commotion around them.
He had acted immediately and without hesitation simply to shield her from humiliation.
Camus’s heart pounded as she stared at him in shock.
It was her first real defeat, her first encounter with someone her own age who could unsettle her so completely, and the first time someone had protected her without asking anything in return.
Too many unfamiliar feelings arrived at once.
For the first time, Camus felt as though the world around her had stopped.
She simply stared at him.
And somewhere in the back of her mind, she was suddenly very glad she had proposed the marriage-alliance idea before leaving Morgue.
Then came another surprise.
The boy was made to face her uncle Adolf.
Camus had always adored her uncle, but for once she thought he was being unfair.
‘He is an adult. Why is he pushing a child this hard?’
She normally appreciated Adolf treating her like an adult, but at that moment she wanted him to show the boy some restraint.
Yet,
‘that boy’ continued to challenge Adolf.
He refused to retreat despite the enormous difference in experience and power.
Camus felt strangely conflicted.
She knew exactly how powerful her uncle was, yet her eyes kept drifting back to the boy.
To her own surprise, she found herself hoping for the absurd outcome—that he might somehow defeat Adolf.
Then,
the impossible became real.
In a situation even Camus had considered hopeless, the boy found a way to pass Adolf’s test.
Splash—
The water jar shattered, water poured down, and Adolf stood there stunned.
Camus cheered before she could stop herself.
She even jumped up and down.
Her uncle looked shocked and disappointed, but for once Camus did not care.
She had always despised the noisy excitement of ordinary children her age, yet without noticing it she was laughing and jumping exactly like one of them.
After the negotiations concluded successfully,
Camus returned home and went to bed.
The stuffed toy she normally hugged while sleeping was set aside.
‘I am not a child anymore.’
Instead, she had a new keepsake.
A small Baskerville cloak.
The cloak the boy had wrapped around her became Camus’s most treasured possession.
She slept with it nearby every night, holding it close as she drifted off.
The familiar scent of cloth and old sweat became oddly comforting to her.
Sometimes merely remembering that day made her heart race; at other times she caught herself gripping the cloak without knowing why.
Whenever her nanny suggested washing it, Camus firmly refused.
“Do not wash this.”
And so it remained untouched.
Seasons changed, several birthdays passed, and Camus kept wondering the same things.
‘Where is he now? What is he doing? How tall has he grown?’
The little girl gradually became a young lady.
She visited the ruby mines frequently under one excuse or another, but never saw him there.
All she heard were scattered rumors that he had left on a long training assignment.
More time passed.
Then, one day, Camus heard a new rumor.
There was a large frontier city on Baskerville territory near Morgue lands.
A remarkably young Deputy Magistrate had been appointed there.
Soon after his arrival, he caused one enormous incident after another.
They were called incidents, but in truth they were extraordinary achievements.
When Camus heard about the ten-billion-gold stake, she slapped her knee.
‘You really did grow up.’
She immediately went to Raspane.
“You want me to assign you to the joint operation with Baskerville?”
“Yes, Mother. Immediately.”
Camus pressed Raspane until the family head agreed.
Before anything else, she made certain that Underdog City’s new Deputy Magistrate would be named as her counterpart in the joint operation.
And the day before the Baskerville delegation was scheduled to arrive,
Camus completed every preparation.
She chose a watchtower surrounded by blooming cotton as the place where they would meet.
Embarrassed by how much the meeting mattered to her, she kept Morgue’s more inquisitive eyes as far away as possible.
Among the white blossoms and their delicate scent, her seven-year wait would finally end.
Camus went to sleep that night full of anticipation.
…And then the day came.
Camus was furious.
The carefully chosen meeting place had already been wrecked.
Her older sisters—who had once tormented her and now barely dared breathe wrong in her presence—had ruined everything.
“What exactly do you think you are doing?”
Camus asked with frightening anger.
The aftermath of the clash was terrible.
Morgue’s three sisters could barely explain themselves.
“C-Camus, we’re your sisters…”
“W-We only wanted to keep the intruders out…”
“They started it!”
But Camus was already in the worst possible mood.
The reunion she had waited seven years for had been turned into a battlefield.
“Camus? Did you just call me by name, sisters?”
“H-Hic! N-No, Deputy Fortress Commander!”
“Move. Unless you want to end up like that.”
Camus did not actually intend to kill the three sisters, but she fully intended to make them regret ruining the day.
She did not need to; the three wisely fled on their own.
Then,
Camus reined in her horse and rode toward ‘him.’
“Welcome back, partner.”
After seven years, his face was still recognizably the same.
He was still growing and was not much taller than she was, but his features had sharpened.
His jaw was more defined, his nose more prominent.
Reality was somehow better than the version Camus had polished in her imagination.
‘You really have grown. Good.’
Camus nodded, and warmth rose to her face on its own.
The embarrassing childhood incident suddenly flashed back through her mind.
‘…You really have changed a lot, haven’t you?’
She had not cast any fire magic, yet Camus suddenly felt far too warm.
