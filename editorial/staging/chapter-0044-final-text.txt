Morgue’s frontier fortress came into view.
A massive earthen wall formed a ring, punctuated by wooden and iron watchtowers.
Camus and Vikir walked around the fortress, talking as they went.
“The barbarians raid the ruby mines and steal native slaves and crops. They are not rescuing their own people; they take captives to sell elsewhere.”
“Exactly. There are countless barbarian tribes, and they do not consider one another kin. They have no hesitation about selling criminals from their own tribe or captives from another.”
“Good. Then I do not have to answer some stupid question about why barbarians fight each other.”
Camus gestured toward the earthen wall ahead.
“Earth mages and iron mages built this wall in a single month.”
The scale of it was enormous.
If ordinary laborers had built it, it would have taken a year rather than a month.
Hundreds of people would have been required.
Vikir moved closer to the wall.
Then he noticed something unusual.
A lattice of steel supports had been embedded inside the hardened earth, visible only because of the holes punched through the wall.
The earthen wall was riddled with holes like the surface of a cracker.
There were hundreds of them.
Vikir studied their size.
Each was roughly three centimeters across.
“…Arrow marks.”
Vikir’s sharp eyes recognized the work of the famous tribe beyond Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain.
Camus nodded.
“The Ballak are the worst. Their arrows carry powerful aura, and many guards have died to them at night. They can punch through two or three meters of packed earth. How are you supposed to stop that?”
“Even shield magic would have trouble. They are too fast.”
“Right. My uncle nearly got himself killed by one not long ago.”
Camus gave a small laugh.
Not long ago, Adolf Morgue himself had been sniped while conducting a search.
“I heard the arrow punched through his defensive magic and badly wounded his chest. He survived, but his pride took quite a hit.”
“Come to think of it, Hugo had a similar experience. I heard the scar on the bridge of his nose came from one of those arrows.”
Vikir remembered the scar across Hugo’s nose.
Archery capable of wounding Swordmaster Hugo and Sixth-Circle Master Adolf was certainly worth fearing.
“There must be someone exceptional among the barbarians. Who is it?”
“I think it is a woman, but the distance was too great to identify her. They also blacken their faces, so she was difficult to remember.”
Camus waved a hand in irritation.
“Anyway, the Ballak are the greatest threat despite numbering only around three hundred. The Rokoko are dangerous too, but their numbers are roughly ten times larger.”
The Ballak were a fiercely warlike tribe.
They lived freely beyond the frontier, owing allegiance to no imperial power, and frequently raided or fought.
For reasons no one understood, they had moved eastward during the last seven years and had begun clashing with Baskerville more often.
Since Morgue had recently leased part of Baskerville’s territory for the ruby development, the Ballak had become Morgue’s problem as well.
Camus looked toward the distant horizon.
“Morgue keeps watch, but barbarian raids are so stealthy that detecting them is difficult. Worse, every month there are a few days when our defenses thin out.”
“A gap?”
Vikir asked, and Camus frowned.
“Morgue is matriarchal. Women overwhelmingly hold authority, and most of the mages guarding the border are women.”
“What does that have to do with a monthly gap?”
“Well… there are always a few days each month when women have certain difficulties.”
“But you are mages, aren’t you?”
Vikir asked. Camus stared at him for a moment, then burst out laughing.
“You really are slow about some things, aren’t you?”
“…?”
“Good. I like it. Men are more fun when they are not too clever. Bonus points.”
Only after Camus patted his shoulder several times did Vikir understand what she meant.
Just then,
“Deputy Fortress Commander!”
Someone called to Camus from a distance.
A female mage rushed over and knelt before her.
“The Dark Hall search party captured a barbarian scout alive!”
They had taken a prisoner.
◆◆◆
The man dragged in bound with rope had brown skin and black hair.
From the tattoos covering his body, Vikir could identify his tribe.
“He is Rokoko—the shaman tribe.”
However he had been captured, his fate was already decided.
Camus Morgue
faced the prisoner with a frightening pressure.
“Did you get anything out of him?”
The nearby mages looked uneasy.
“He refuses to speak.”
“What about mind magic?”
“It does not work. A powerful curse prevents us from reading his memories.”
Camus turned.
Then she walked over and stopped in front of the captive.
“You raided this fortress before and carried away slaves.”
“….”
“One of them was a girl of House Morgue. My adopted younger sister. Her name is Rosie.”
Camus stared at the Rokoko prisoner with burning eyes.
“What did you do to her?”
The prisoner’s tightly closed mouth slowly opened.
“Anna.”
Camus frowned at the word.
“Translate that. Where are the barbarian defectors?”
No one answered.
The mages merely exchanged uneasy looks.
One of them spoke apologetically.
“They were all killed or taken when the barbarians attacked, Deputy Fortress Commander.”
“Then no one here can translate his words?”
“Not at the moment.”
It was an awkward problem.
Everyone looked uncertain.
“I know a little Rokoko.”
Vikir stepped forward.
Camus stared at him in surprise.
“You can do that too? Is there anything you cannot do?”
“Not well. I only know a few basic words.”
Vikir moved beside Camus.
Camus said,
“Ask where my adopted younger sister is. She was taken in the last raid. Red hair, red eyes, unusually pale skin, around twelve years old.”
Vikir nodded, then addressed the Rokoko captive.
“?? ???? ?? ??????”
A short reply came back.
“???”
Vikir’s expression hardened for an instant.
Then he turned to Camus and shook his head.
“She is dead.”
Every Morgue mage’s expression darkened.
They had known death was likely after an abduction, but hearing it confirmed was different.
Camus stepped forward.
She growled at the captive in a low voice.
“When this war is over, your language will be spoken only in hell.”
Those were the final words the prisoner heard.
Camus spoke to the assembled mages.
“The Light Hall delegate is being treated, and the Dark Hall delegate is inspecting the opposite sector. I, Camus Morgue, councilor and Deputy Fortress Commander, will pass judgment here.”
That concluded the summary trial.
And then,
Camus traced a magic circle through the air with one hand.
A moment later,
Boom!
A huge iron skewer erupted from the earth.
Iron hidden throughout the soil gathered under her magic and exploded upward, impaling the Rokoko prisoner in one stroke.
From groin to crown.
The prisoner convulsed, unable even to scream.
He hung high above the ground, nailed to the iron skewer.
Flames began to roar beneath him.
Crackle!
The iron skewer became an execution stake, and Camus burned the Rokoko captive to death in moments.
The slaves who witnessed it stared at Camus in fear.
The people of Morgue were little different.
Crackle, crackle, crackle!
The sound of a body burning on the skewer filled the air.
Black ash scattered on the wind with the smell of scorched flesh.
In front of that terrible fire, Camus smiled calmly.
“Let’s go.”
She pulled Vikir away with her, and the two left at once.
Everyone around them could only watch their backs with faint dread.
◆◆◆
Meanwhile,
once they were behind the earthen wall, Vikir found himself slightly surprised.
It was not because he had watched a man be impaled alive and burned.
Before regression, Vikir had spent decades on battlefields and had seen far worse.
What surprised him was Camus’s expression.
“…Ugh… hic…!”
Once they reached a place with no one around, Camus began to cry.
Her face twisted, her eyes reddened, and tears streamed down her round cheeks.
Vikir’s mouth fell slightly open in disbelief.
He had never expected to see the future Ironblood Empress crying like this.
He had seen her cry at eight, but seeing it now that she was fifteen felt very different.
‘She is still only fifteen.’
Vikir watched the crying Camus for a while before finally speaking.
“…Why are you crying?”
“Why would I be crying!”
Camus snapped, then glanced around to make sure no one could hear.
Vikir closed his mouth for a moment, then tried again.
“You must have been very close to your sister.”
“I was. She always followed me around. She was innocent and kind—far too gentle for House Morgue.”
Camus crouched against the earthen wall.
They were nearly the same height, yet she somehow looked much smaller now.
Vikir thought for a moment.
“Do not grieve too much. She must have gone peacefully.”
At Vikir’s awkward attempt to comfort her, Camus shot back angrily.
“Do you think I’m stupid?”
The question carried both anger and grief.
Vikir understood at once.
Camus had understood the Rokoko captive.
“I am a genius. I cannot speak Rokoko, but I can understand some of it.”
“….”
“Tell me the truth. Did I hear him correctly?”
Vikir could only nod heavily.
The Rokoko prisoner’s final word had not meant “dead.”
“Eaten.”
The Rokoko were a tribe of shamans—and cannibals.
Their custom was to eat captives.
When Vikir confirmed it, Camus’s tears began again.
“…I’m sorry. I’m sorry I could not protect you. Your big sister is sorry.”
Camus cried openly.
Vikir simply stood beside her without speaking.
He had not expected Camus Morgue—the future Ironblood Empress, queen of red and black, of fire and skewers—to hide such a side behind her mask.
…After some time,
Camus rose.
She wiped the dried tracks from her cheeks with her sleeve.
Her usual cold expression returned.
She looked at Vikir standing silently nearby.
“That was not bad.”
“…?”
“If you had offered me some flimsy sympathy, I might have killed you.”
A fifteen-year-old girl’s threat meant little to a veteran who had survived decades of war, but Vikir gave a grave nod anyway.
…Even so,
sometimes simply staying beside someone was comfort enough.
Vikir had not known what to say to a crying fifteen-year-old, and this time his silence had turned out to be the right choice.
Camus patted Vikir on the chest.
“No time to mourn. We need to recover quickly and take revenge.”
“….”
“Come with me. There are things we need to do together.”
Camus sounded utterly determined.
