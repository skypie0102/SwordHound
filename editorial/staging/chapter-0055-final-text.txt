The Ballak were a warlike people by nature.
Take war and hunting out of their lives, and there would hardly be anything left.
As Vikir followed Aiyen toward the village entrance, he reviewed what he knew about the Ballak.
‘Their language has only about three hundred words, most of them nouns. Verbs are often conveyed through gestures, and possession barely distinguishes “mine” from “ours.” A man is ambo, a woman akouati, love aauki, death utika…’
The Ballak regarded Imperials as rude, deceitful, and pretentious.
Imperials, in turn, regarded the Ballak as barbarians who shouted and threw punches at the slightest provocation.
Vikir thought the people of Baskerville stood somewhere between the two: neither fully Imperial nor fully of the jungle.
‘Perhaps living on the border made them resemble both sides without truly belonging to either.’
While Vikir was thinking, Ballak’s hunters began gathering in a circle.
Hunters young and old formed small groups of three or four.
Some climbed onto wolves with bows and spears at the ready.
Only Aiyen had no hunting companions beside her—just one wolf and her slave, Vikir.
“Gather around, hunters. It is time for the ritual.”
A crooked old man stepped forward carrying a bowl of black charcoal and began smearing it over the hunters’ faces.
Bright tattoos showed beneath his dark robes; he appeared to be one of the tribe’s shamans.
“Receive my blessing before you leave, unless you want misfortune to strike.”
The old shaman dipped his fingers into the charcoal mixture and marked each hunter’s face.
Ballak hunters believed that taking prey carried a spiritual burden.
Before a kill, they blackened their faces so the god of death would not recognize them.
The younger hunters, Aiyen included, did not seem especially fond of the custom.
“There he goes again with the nagging.”
“Ignore him. A shaman has to preserve his authority somehow.”
“Then why are his predictions always wrong?”
“That is why his fortunes can mean one thing or the exact opposite.”
Young people seemed to dislike old customs and superstitions no matter where or when they lived.
Aiyen reluctantly leaned forward and let the shaman apply the charcoal.
He gave her only a few perfunctory strokes before moving on, far less carefully than with the others; the two clearly did not get along.
Next Aiyen put on a spiked collar and handed another to Vikir.
“Wear this. It keeps beasts from getting their teeth around your neck.”
Vikir nodded.
One by one, the hunters began leaving the village.
Knives, spears, and bows in hand, they mounted their wolves and headed out.
Vikir made his own preparations as well.
‘If I am staying here, I should learn to blend in.’
He needed to adjust his habits to life among the Ballak.
Remembering what Aiyen had taught him about local customs, Vikir tried to imitate what he thought was normal village behavior.
He decided that even small habits would help him adapt.
Then something went wrong.
The Ballak around him reacted at once, staring in surprise.
Some looked away while others stared openly, making it obvious Vikir had misunderstood the custom.
Confused, Vikir asked what was wrong.
“Why is everyone staring? I thought this was normal here.”
Replies came from every direction.
“…What are you talking about? Who does that wherever they please?”
“Is that how people behave in the Empire? How barbaric.”
“We have designated latrines.”
“Men use that side. Women use the other.”
“At least show some manners.”
“Seriously, how uncivilized.”
“What did you think we meant?”
“….”
Vikir slowly turned toward Aiyen.
Aiyen smirked and immediately looked away.
◆◆◆
Aiyen climbed onto the wolf’s back.
“Hold on tight.”
Vikir climbed on behind her.
Unsure where to brace himself, he first grabbed handfuls of the wolf’s fur.
Aiyen reached back, caught his arm, and pulled it around her waist.
“Hold here, and hold tight. If you do not, you will fall and break your neck.”
Vikir adjusted his grip around her waist as instructed, taking care not to lose his balance.
But the warning came too late.
Thump!
The wolf sprang forward once, and Vikir went tumbling to the ground.
Pain shot through him so sharply that he nearly cried out.
His bones, only barely knitted together by Murcielago’s regeneration, twisted painfully again.
Riding a wolf was nothing like riding a horse.
There was neither saddle nor stirrup.
A wolf’s back was narrower than a horse’s, and its movements were far more abrupt.
Aiyen stopped the wolf and jumped down.
“Tsk. I told you to wrap both arms around my waist and hold on. A loose grip will throw you right off.”
She patted her waist with an amused look, clearly telling him to stop being so hesitant.
Vikir was just beginning to stand.
Thud!
Someone moved between Vikir and Aiyen.
It was Ahun, looking down at Vikir from the back of his wolf.
The atmosphere chilled at once.
Aiyen folded her arms, visibly displeased.
Competition among Ballak hunters was constant.
Ahun, who had long competed with Aiyen, challenged her again over the coming hunt.
“This time I will bring back the larger prey. Then I will lead the next joint hunting party. I swear it on my grandfather’s honor.”
“Your grandfather is a shaman. Why wager his honor on a hunting contest? Do whatever you want.”
Aiyen showed little interest in Ahun’s challenge.
She merely motioned for Vikir to come over.
Vikir had just regained his feet and started toward her.
Smack!
His head snapped sideways.
Ahun had ridden past and kicked Vikir in the face.
Still badly injured, Vikir had seen it coming but could not move quickly enough to evade.
Fortunately, Ahun had not put much force into the blow.
Aiyen’s expression immediately hardened with fury.
“You little—what do you think you’re doing?”
She started forward, then stopped abruptly.
Her gaze shifted between Vikir on the ground and Ahun on his wolf.
Ahun flinched at her reaction, then seemed to relax.
He looked down at Vikir and sneered.
“How is a weakling like that supposed to help you hunt? Wake up, Captain. He will only drag you down.”
“Before saying that, you should check your own waist.”
At Aiyen’s dry reply, Ahun looked down.
His quiver—the one that should have been full of arrows—was gone.
“…!?”
Ahun spun around.
Vikir was already back on his feet, holding the missing quiver and arrows.
He had quietly lifted them from Ahun just before hitting the ground.
‘Another skill I owe to the Age of Destruction.’
Before the regression, there had been almost no kind of fieldwork Vikir had not been forced to learn.
Even pickpocketing had its uses.
“A weapon is like a lover. If you let someone steal it, they can use it against you.”
Vikir’s solemn delivery made Aiyen laugh and Ahun flush with anger.
“…You insolent slave!”
Ahun swung his bow into Vikir’s shoulder.
Crack!
Vikir fell again under the blow.
Ahun dismounted from his wolf as well.
Whoosh!
He raised the bow for another strike.
Clack!
The bow stopped in midair.
Aiyen had intercepted it with an arrow.
“Enough. Whose slave do you think you are punishing?”
Her voice was cold and hard.
Ahun stammered out an objection.
“He is a slave! How dare he insult a Ballak warrior?”
“You embarrassed yourself first.”
“Even so, he is still a slave. A slave has no right to insult a warrior!”
Ahun appealed to tribal law.
“….”
Aiyen considered it, then nodded.
“Fine. A slave is still a slave. If he does wrong, he can be punished.”
“Exactly, Captain.”
“But you do not get to punish him. He is my slave.”
“…?”
Private property meant little among the Ballak, but ownership of slaves was clearly defined.
When Ahun hesitated, Aiyen made the point explicit.
“If he deserves punishment, I will administer it myself.”
She slid down from the wolf and walked over to Vikir.
Then she raised her hand.
Smack!
Aiyen struck Vikir across the cheek.
When he went down, she continued the punishment herself.
Her blows landed on his torso and legs rather than his already damaged head.
Vikir curled up and endured them without a sound.
After a while Aiyen stepped back, breathing hard.
Then she fixed Ahun with a cold stare.
“Satisfied?”
“….”
Under Aiyen’s glare, Ahun quickly nodded.
She kept her eyes on him.
“I know you still resent my slave for throwing you from your wolf and humiliating you during the raid.”
“…Yes.”
“From now on, if you harass my slave in secret, be prepared to receive the same treatment. Understood?”
Blood trickled from Aiyen’s clenched fist.
Her eyes carried unmistakable killing intent.
Ahun nodded stiffly, then gave the motionless Vikir one last look.
Vikir had not screamed once; he simply lay there in silence.
Ahun mounted his wolf and left at once.
Once again, only Aiyen and Vikir remained.
Aiyen let out a quiet breath.
She walked over and crouched in front of him.
Vikir slowly pushed himself upright.
With a complicated expression, Aiyen touched his reddened cheek.
“…Did that hurt?”
Her tone sounded subtly different from before, though Vikir could not tell whether he was imagining it.
