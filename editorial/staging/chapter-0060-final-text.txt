The Plunder Festival.
A great celebration filled the Ballak village.
The Ballak found reasons to celebrate almost every day, but a day when meat was plentiful was louder than most.
Fresh water poured from cut jungle vines and collected in a great stone vessel.
Crayfish, freshwater crabs, turtles, carp, catfish, venison, rabbit, squirrel, snake, insects, frogs, lizards, greens, berries, mushrooms, and roots all went into the boiling water.
A huge stone pot bubbled at the center of the village.
The smaller game brought back by the hunters simmered together into a rich communal broth.
Everyone in the village received a fair portion.
Larger animals such as deer and boar were roasted separately around the fires.
Villagers gathered around the great cuts of meat, singing and dancing as they ate.
Yet the prize that drew the most attention was the enormous carcass at the center of the settlement.
An oxbear—a massive horned beast weighing well over a ton.
Once hide and bones were removed, the edible meat still came to more than eight hundred kilograms.
That oxbear was the unquestioned centerpiece of the day’s hunt.
The hunter who brought down such prey earned the respect and gratitude of the entire village.
This time, that honor belonged to Aiyen and Vikir.
“How does it feel to be the hero of the feast, slave?”
Vikir gave Aiyen no particular answer.
She laughed and patted him on the back.
“An oxbear is honorable prey. Strong, fierce, and clever—not like a boar that panics, charges blindly, and makes a mess of itself when frightened.”
Aiyen deliberately said it loudly enough for nearby hunters to hear.
A disgruntled noise immediately came from the other side of the roasting fire.
Ahun stood there beside the large boar he had killed.
“…Unbelievable. I still don’t know how someone half-dead managed to bring back an oxbear. You must have burned every scrap of luck you had.”
Ahun spat to one side and glared at Vikir.
Then he took up his knife and began portioning the oxbear.
Blood and organs spoiled quickly, so they were transferred to pots at once, while the flesh was cut into shares.
Ahun passed portions down the waiting line until Vikir’s turn arrived.
…?
Vikir frowned at what he had been given.
His portion was noticeably smaller than everyone else’s.
Even frail elders and small children had received more meat.
Vikir looked up.
“What are you doing?”
“…?”
Ahun stared back as though the question made no sense.
Then he answered with open annoyance.
“What are you complaining about, slave?”
“Why is my portion so small?”
“What? Obviously because your honor is so high!”
Ahun barked the answer.
Vikir looked genuinely confused, while Aiyen broke into a grin beside him.
“It is Ballak custom. The hunter who contributes the most takes the least meat. What he gives up in food returns to him as honor. If you could trade honor back into meat, you would be rich.”
Ahun nodded reluctantly.
“Damn it. Part of me wants to pile your plate high too. But credit is credit.”
From the way he grumbled, he apparently meant it.
Among the Ballak, less meat meant more honor.
Vikir privately considered actual meat more useful than abstract honor, but there was no point arguing with the custom.
‘It does not matter much.’
Even with a small formal share, the feast contained more than enough food if he wanted it.
And meat was not the real reward of the hunt anyway.

The oxbear kill had supplied Beelzebub with a new skill.
One of the three orbs now held the hardy power of the male oxbear slain in the Cold Valley.
A weaker stored spirit had been displaced as the higher-ranked oxbear ability took its place.
Sacral Spine was simple but extremely practical.
While holding his breath and activating it, Vikir could increase his effective body weight by roughly six hundred kilograms.
That had obvious applications in close combat.
The karma and experience from killing the oxbear had also flowed into Vikir, accelerating the recovery of his battered body.
A new skill and more experience.
Vikir quietly calculated the true value of the hunt.
“Come on. That was a fine kill, wasn’t it?”
Aiyen’s voice interrupted his thoughts.
She had brought several kilograms of foreleg meat, a jar of blood, and select organs traditionally reserved for the most successful hunters.
Aiyen held the cuts up in front of him.
Her expression turned unusually serious.
“Bringing prey down is only half the hunt. Butchering and preserving it properly matters just as much.”
Aiyen plainly intended to use the work as another opportunity to hover nearby and supervise him.
There was one thing she did not know.
During the Age of Destruction, Vikir had processed countless monster carcasses; separating organs, meat, bones, and hide for food or materials was second nature.
Scrape, scrape, scrape…
Vikir soaked the hide in a broad bamboo vat and removed the remaining fat with a sharp stone knife.
The skin came away cleanly and was set aside for tanning.
Once dry, such hides became board-stiff, but repeated beating with oil restored flexibility.
The oxbear’s flesh, organs, and blood were boiled; rendered fat floating to the top was skimmed away for lamps and cooking.
Bones, claws, and teeth were saved for tools, weapons, and ornaments.
The meat itself was prepared in many different ways.
Some became jerky, some soup or stew, some was lightly poached, some smoked over coals, and some hung up to dry.
Vikir was especially skilled at smoking large cuts whole.
People watching him work gradually began bringing their own portions for him to prepare.
Aiyen, who had initially come to teach him, ended up among them.
Vikir tied a large salted cut with rope and suspended it from a branch.
He dug a pit beneath it, then piled pine needles and aromatic hardwood at the bottom and lit a controlled fire.
Once enough smoke was rising, he sealed the pit with branches, leaves, and wet earth.
Later, he broke through the hardened mud cap and pulled up the rope, revealing meat browned deeply by smoke.
An entire oxbear hind leg had been smoked through.
The Ballak villagers reacted with immediate enthusiasm.
Aiyen’s eyes widened when she tasted it.
“What can’t you do?”
For someone with Vikir’s accumulated lifetime of experience, none of these skills seemed remarkable, though they were startling in a fifteen-year-old body.
Vikir watched children blow impatiently on hot pieces of meat before taking eager bites.
The uncomplicated excitement of children was difficult not to appreciate, regardless of one’s age.
He watched the village divide and eat the food he had prepared.
Ballak food-sharing included a peculiar medicinal custom.
Those with poor eyesight preferred the oxbear’s eyes.
Those with lung trouble ate its lungs.
People with weak legs sought meat from the legs.
Those with digestive trouble chose organs associated with digestion.
The tribe believed that eating the corresponding part of powerful prey could strengthen an ailing body part.
‘A strange superstition.’
That was Vikir’s assessment.
“Hey, slave.”
A voice called from nearby.
Aiyen, who had disappeared during the butchering, returned behind him.
Her face was warm from the fires, and she carried a large coconut-shell bowl.
A thick, steaming organ broth bubbled inside.
A long, dense piece of meat floated in the oily soup, difficult to identify after cooking.
“An organ?”
Vikir could not tell which one through the rich broth.
Aiyen sniffed proudly.
“I claimed a restorative cut the shaman wanted. You are eating this.”
“I already received meat, blood, and organ portions.”
“No. This one is specifically for you.”
Aiyen pushed the bowl insistently into Vikir’s hands.
Her reasoning was simple.
“If someone’s eyes are weak, they eat eyes. If their heart is weak, they eat heart. If their limbs are weak, they eat the corresponding meat.”
“Then what exactly is this supposed to strengthen?”
Aiyen coughed and refused to identify the long piece in the broth.
“You’ve been injured, sleeping in the cold, and exhausting yourself. Just treat it as strengthening food and eat.”
She mumbled the explanation and looked away.
Nearby villagers immediately noticed the special treatment.
“Oho! Captain is already saving the prized cuts for her own slave!”
“Who gives a personal restorative portion to a slave?”
“Winter nights are getting longer. Better keep your useful people healthy.”
“She could not make the favoritism more obvious if she tried.”
Several young women laughed and booed Aiyen in unison.
“Quiet! If you are jealous, go catch your own oxbear and choose your own share!”
Aiyen raised a fist, and the teasing quickly subsided.
“….”
Vikir frowned, still unsure why everyone found the situation so amusing.
Food was food.
He drank the hot broth.
At Aiyen’s insistence, he finished the dense organ meat as well.
“This is extremely tough.”
The texture reminded him somewhat of Baskerville haggis, though it was richer and far chewier.
Gulp.
Vikir swallowed the final piece.
Only then did Aiyen’s tense expression relax.
“Good. Now I have no reason to worry that you’ll collapse before the next hunt.”
Vikir frowned again.
“I think I already contributed more than enough to one hunt.”
He still could not tell what additional plans she was making for him.
