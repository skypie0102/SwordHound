The Hound of the Night had returned.
By the time Vikir reached Ballak’s village, the Red Death had spread throughout the surrounding lands.
Ballak’s floating shelters, drainage work, and flood control had limited the damage, but neighboring tribes had suffered terribly.
Ahun was the first person to run out when he heard Vikir had returned.
With tears streaming down his face, he threw himself at Vikir.
“You came back!”
Ahun had barely eaten or slept for days.
Since Aheul fell ill, he had done little except remain beside her and care for her.
He seized Vikir’s hand with both of his.
“Thank you for returning. Even if you couldn’t find the cure, the fact that you kept your promise is enough.”
Because Vikir appeared empty-handed, Ahun assumed he had failed.
He had not.
Vikir had brought a miracle capable of curing his friends in the depths.
“Follow me.”
“…?”
Vikir pulled the stunned Ahun toward his shelter.
Aheul lay inside, still suffering from the illness that had struck her first and hardest.
She had grown terribly thin, though Ahun’s careful tending had kept her clean and comfortable.
Without hesitation, Vikir produced the vial containing Saintess’s Tears.
Pow!
The vial was still sealed, yet powerful holy light radiated from within.
Even through the container, that light began driving the Red Death from Aheul’s body.
The red spots faded.
The disease that had tormented her for days receded with astonishing ease.
“…! …! …! …!”
Ahun stared, crying too hard to speak.
His frozen posture said everything.
He reached out with a trembling hand and touched Aheul’s face.
Her expression, twisted by pain until moments before, relaxed for the first time in days.
Her breathing grew steadier.
“…Vikir.”
Ahun said the name once, almost reverently, then repeated it.
“Vikir! Vikir! Vikir! Vikir!”
The trust, relief, and gratitude in his voice made the chant sound almost like a prayer.
The hunters closest to Ahun began repeating it too.
They had all watched helplessly while someone close to them suffered.
Now the impossible weight of that helplessness had vanished in front of them.
“Vikir! Vikir! Vikir! Vikir! Vikir!”
Ballak’s warriors stamped their feet and shouted loudly enough to shake the floating shelter.
Vikir remained calm.
One patient had been saved, but the outbreak was far from over.
He turned toward Ahun and the others.
“How many patients are there?”
“About thirty in Ballak, including Aheul.”
Vikir nodded.
That was still a small enough number to contain quickly.
But no one could afford to relax yet.
Vikir intended not merely to disrupt Leviathan’s presumed scheme, but to seize every consequence of it for his own future strategy.
“What about the other tribes?”
Ahun’s expression darkened.
“It’s terrible. Tribes that ignored the flood-control advice were hit hardest. Those who built higher shelters did better, but not enough.”
“How many sick?”
“I don’t know. More than I could count. Whole settlements are full of them.”
The total was probably in the tens of thousands.
Vikir made his decision.
“Bring the sick from the other tribes here as well.”
“What? All of them? Do we have enough cure?”
“Don’t worry about that. This is also a chance to build trust with the other tribes.”
Ahun and the younger warriors immediately brightened.
Helping rival tribes was not as strange to them as an Imperial might assume.
Marriage, hunting, and territory caused frequent conflict, but blood ties and old alliances still connected many jungle peoples.
In a crisis, those bonds often mattered more than old grudges.
Ballak’s warriors hurried out to spread the news.
Meanwhile—
“….”
Vikir looked at the vial of Saintess’s Tears.
“This can become leverage against Baskerville as well.”
He intended to use the disaster to draw the Black Mountain tribes closer together.
Baskerville’s traditional mission was expansion into and suppression of the Black Mountains.
Its most persistent enemies were not necessarily the other Great Families.
They were the peoples who actually lived beyond the frontier.
Ballak, Rokoko, and countless smaller tribes had resisted Baskerville’s advance for generations.
They crossed forests that would tear ordinary soldiers apart.
Their archers could hit tiny targets from impossible ranges, and their hunters could bring down monsters with axes, bows, and spears.
That was ordinary life in Le Rouge Et Le Noir Mountain.
If Vikir could earn the loyalty of those tribes, he would gain a force capable of pressuring Baskerville from the frontier itself.
If the plague were stopped early and the tribes grew stronger rather than weaker, that pressure would only increase.
“So we need to stay on good terms with them.”
Vikir was already Ballak’s hero.
Now he intended to become someone the entire jungle remembered.
Whatever Leviathan had intended, the Red Death was creating opportunities Vikir could use.
“We’re short on time. Move before the death toll rises.”
Using the radiance of Saintess’s Tears, Vikir treated every infected person in Ballak.
The next task was cleansing the shared water source.
The concentrated holy water needed to reach the upper rivers that fed the flooded lowlands.
Vikir moved immediately.
After two years in the jungle, he already knew where the major headwaters lay.
The entire tribe went with him.
Representatives from other groups also began arriving, including survivors and enemies who normally would never share the same path.
By the time Akwilla led them to the source, hunters and shamans from several peoples had gathered together.
“All of you may drink from this water.”
Vikir released Saintess’s Tears into the river.
A brilliant light spread through the current.
For a moment the river looked like a strip of the Milky Way had fallen from the night sky.
The watching tribespeople stared, transfixed.
Then they began scooping water into their hands.
A miracle followed.
The Red Death’s influence began disappearing from the water and from the sick gathered nearby.
“Ohhhhh!”
A roar of relief swept through the jungle.
Akwilla herself shouted with delight.
“Vikir, at this rate I should make you our shaman!”
The Night Fox openly praised the Night Hound.
People from every tribe called out to Vikir in their own languages.
Their words and gestures differed, but the meaning was the same.
Respect, gratitude, and relief spread through the crowd.
The sick, their families, and those who had watched friends suffer all bowed before him.
For that moment, the gratitude of the jungle converged on Vikir.
Even elders who had once distrusted the Imperial outsider lowered their heads.
Many were thanking him for sons, daughters, partners, and grandchildren who would now survive.
Meanwhile—
“…?”
Even while treating patient after patient, Vikir kept looking for someone.
He had been told that Aiyen was ill, yet she was nowhere among the sick.
He searched the crowd again and again.
Then—
Ta-ta-ta-ta-ta.
Rapid footsteps approached.
From the sound alone, Vikir knew exactly who it was.
He turned.
Puck!
Someone slammed into him at full speed.
Aiyen wrapped both arms around Vikir.
Thud!
The impact was strong enough to make his muscles tense and bones complain.
Vikir instinctively reinforced himself with mana before the embrace could injure him.
Then he caught Aiyen by the cheeks and pulled her face back far enough to inspect her.
“I heard you had the Red Death.”
But Aiyen looked perfectly healthy.
There were no red spots, no fever sweat, and no sign of illness.
The only unusual thing was the warm flush on her face.
To be safe, Vikir dabbed a little holy water against her skin, but the blush remained.
Aiyen looked down at herself in confusion.
“What…?”
She scratched her head.
Then she looked back at Vikir.
“I feel better now. Completely better.”
