After hearing the report, Aiyen frowned.
“…She is still out there?”
For two full years, Camus Morgue had continued searching across the frontier.
Officially, her expeditions were for barbarian suppression and territorial surveys, but almost no one knew her real purpose.
Almost no one except Aiyen.
‘Obvious.’
Aiyen did not need to ask.
Camus was searching for the man who had disappeared two years earlier.
Vikir.
Aiyen remembered the first time she had met him.
He had been the small boy who saved her from dying in a cage, and she had never forgotten his face.
When she met him again, however, another woman had been in his arms.
That woman was Camus, and she too had remembered Vikir enough to return again and again to this miserable frontier.
Camus seemed determined to find him alive, or at least recover his body.
She kept searching with a thin strand of hope she refused to let go.
Two years of persistence were enough to make even Aiyen grudgingly respect her determination.
“If you came for rubies, then go back to your rubies. What a troublesome woman.”
Aiyen crossed her arms and fell silent.
She was deciding whether she should tell Vikir what she had learned.
◆◆◆
In the jungle, Aiyen followed Vikir’s trail.
She soon found him ahead, already stalking prey.
Aiyen rode up beside him on Bakira.
Vikir was studying the depth and shape of tracks in the mud.
“Mushuhushu, a water snake. Very old, very large. If we catch it, the whole village can eat for at least three days.”
From the tracks alone, Vikir could estimate the creature’s size, weight, direction, location, age, health, and even its current condition.
Those skills had all come from Ballak’s hunters.
“….”
Aiyen stared at him.
Vikir had changed greatly over the past two years.
His features were still youthful, but his presence had become far more commanding.
Two years earlier he had still seemed like a boy forcing himself through every situation alone, but now experience had settled naturally into him.
The boy was steadily becoming a man.
‘No wonder that woman from Morgue refuses to forget him.’
Aiyen gave a small nod.
As the next leader of Ballak she had every reason to dislike Morgue, but she could still understand Camus on a personal level.
Aiyen opened her mouth.
For some reason, a slight tremor entered her voice.
“…Hey, slave.”
She had never stopped calling Vikir that even after his formal release.
Vikir had long since stopped caring, so he answered without looking back.
“What?”
Aiyen hesitated before asking.
“What was the place you came from like?”
“…?”
Vikir was silent for a moment.
The place he came from.
Did she mean Baskerville—or the world before his regression?
He answered in a way that fit both.
“Hell.”
Aiyen scratched her head.
Then she asked what she had actually wanted to know.
“Do you want to go back?”
“….”
Vikir paused and looked over his shoulder.
He noticed the strange tension in her expression.
“Why are you asking that with a face like that?”
Aiyen looked briefly embarrassed.
Even now she was still hesitating.
Should she tell him that Morgue had continued searching for him?
At last she shut her eyes for a moment.
Then she forced the words out.
“I do not want to hide this from you.”
“…?”
“She is looking for you.”
The words were finally spoken.
Aiyen clenched her fists.
She hated concealment and dishonesty in the first place.
She especially did not want to use either against Vikir.
After speaking, she felt both relieved and strangely frustrated.
What if Vikir said he intended to leave? Could Ballak law even stop him?
Too many thoughts crowded her mind.
Then—
Vikir answered.
“I am not going back.”
The reply was short, and the tension that had bound Aiyen’s body loosened all at once.
“…Really?”
“Yes.”
Vikir nodded.
There was no reason for him to return yet.
He intended to go back only after recovering enough strength to control what Hugo could perceive.
At minimum, he needed to be able to hide his true power perfectly.
“…And who exactly is ‘she’?”
Vikir frowned for a moment.
Was Aiyen talking about Camus Morgue?
If so, he supposed he owed Camus some gratitude for remembering the life-saving debt between them.
‘More loyal than I expected. Or is this some diplomatic move?’
Vikir closed his eyes in thought.
If Morgue was still searching here, then its forces were still operating inside Baskerville’s sphere.
If Baskerville continued allowing those search parties into the depths, then his current family had apparently not forgotten him either.
It also implied that the Baskerville–Morgue alliance had remained intact or even strengthened.
Perhaps their joint annual operations had continued in one form or another.
The outside situation was clearly more complicated than he could see from Ballak territory.
Sooner or later, Vikir knew he would have to leave the depths discreetly and investigate.
Just then, Aiyen spoke again in a noticeably lighter voice.
“There is something else.”
She pulled an object from her clothing.
It was the dagger recovered from the mysterious Imperial intruder.
“Do you recognize this mark?”
A large serpent was engraved on the dagger.
Vikir’s eyes narrowed.
“I know it. It is a famous emblem.”
It belonged to one of the Empire’s great houses.
“House Leviathan.”
Leviathan stood among the Seven Great Families beside Baskerville, Morgue, Quovadis, Bourgeois, and the others.
Why, then, would its mark appear here in the depths?
Vikir’s thoughts accelerated.
“Can I keep this?”
“Of course. Do what you want with it.”
Aiyen handed it over without hesitation.
Vikir took the serpent-marked dagger.
Just then—
[Gurgle… gurgle!]
An unpleasant cry sounded nearby.
Vikir and Aiyen turned toward the muddy mangrove forest.
Something massive crawled among the twisted roots.
It was a giant lungfish.
The smooth, scaleless body dragged itself through the mud while thick mucus coated its skin.
It was over eight meters long, ugly even by jungle standards, and not considered food by Ballak.
Aiyen grimaced.
“The rainy season is definitely close if things like that are already moving.”
“If specimens that large are surfacing, this rainy season may be a long one.”
Vikir agreed.
The mud already carried the deep tracks of giant lungfish.
Aiyen pointed toward an old fallen tree.
High above the current ground level, dried aquatic vegetation still clung to the trunk.
“Last year the floodwater rose that high.”
“It may rise even higher this year.”
When the rains came, water levels could rise with terrifying speed.
The lungfish sensed that change early and emerged from deep mud before the floods arrived.
Then—
“…!”
Aiyen’s sharp eyes caught something.
Something protruded from the body of the lungfish crawling past them.
It was a harpoon.
“Look at that.”
Aiyen moved immediately.
She killed the creature with an arrow, then cut open its belly.
A partly digested human body slid from the stomach.
Aiyen’s expression hardened.
“Rokoko.”
The dead hunter belonged to Ballak’s rival tribe, the second great power of the depths after Ballak.
Its people were feared for black magic, curses, and cannibalistic customs.
Almost every jungle tribe feared Rokoko.
Ballak did not.
Vikir looked at the corpse.
“Does this mean Rokoko has entered Ballak territory for war?”
“I do not think so.”
Aiyen examined the harpoon.
It had not pierced the lungfish from outside.
It had been driven outward from inside the stomach.
The Rokoko hunter had been swallowed first, and the weapon in his hands had torn through the creature from within.
That created another problem.
Lungfish were huge but slow scavengers; they should not have been able to overpower a seasoned Rokoko hunter.
The remains looked like a young man, probably only in his early twenties.
Aiyen frowned.
“How weak would a Rokoko hunter have to be for a lungfish to eat him? These things barely hunt anything alive.”
“Weak enough to be taken.”
“Then why would someone that weak be sent hunting at all?”
It was a reasonable question.
Vikir gave the simplest explanation.
“Because the tribe is desperate enough that even someone in that condition has to hunt.”
It was only a hypothesis.
The hunter might instead have been weakened suddenly by some external cause.
But the body showed no obvious traumatic injury before being swallowed.
Scratch marks inside the lungfish showed that the hunter had struggled, but with very little strength.
“Something is wrong.”
Aiyen’s instincts were screaming at her.
She and Vikir began searching the surrounding area.
Given the state of digestion and the slow speed of a lungfish, traces of the hunter’s earlier path should still remain nearby.
Before long they found them.
While alive, the Rokoko hunter had walked with an unsteady gait.
He had wandered without recognizing that he had crossed into Ballak territory, moving erratically and desperately.
The tracks suggested he had been looking for small prey or even edible fruit rather than normal large game.
That was completely unlike Rokoko’s famously aggressive hunters.
What could have reduced them to this state?
Vikir and Aiyen followed the trail farther.
Eventually they neared Rokoko territory.
“…!?”
Both of them stopped.
A simple campsite lay ahead.
Several Rokoko hunters were gathered there.
Rokoko carried traces of ancient dark-elf ancestry and were famous for striking appearances.
They were equally feared for black magic, curses, and witchcraft.
Those traits also made them frequent targets for slavers.
But neither Vikir nor Aiyen cared about their appearance now.
Every Rokoko hunter in the camp was dead.
