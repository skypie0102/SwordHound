Ahmen was panicking.
His plan had been simple: finish the ritual, release Leviathan’s antidote, cure everyone, and emerge as Ballak’s savior.
He had expected respect and gratitude.
Instead, most of Ballak’s warriors stared at him with open contempt.
“…?”
That reaction confused him.
Even if his ritual had failed, why were the villagers acting as though he had betrayed them?
Failure alone should not have produced this much hostility.
But—
Akwilla’s next words answered the question.
“Enough. I want to see what’s inside the sack at your waist.”
Ahmen’s heart sank.
He barely kept his expression under control.
Why would the chieftain ask about that particular sack now?
There was only one reason she would care.
“…Did they notice?”
For the man who had helped spread the Red Death, the question felt like a knife against his stomach.
Akwilla stared at him.
“What are you waiting for? Show me the sack.”
The chieftain’s authority in Ballak was absolute.
Yet Ahmen was so frightened that he openly resisted.
“I can’t show you that! I can’t!”
He was too flustered to realize what he had just done.
The reaction was immediate.
Before his denial even ended, the surrounding warriors’ faces hardened.
Younger hunters sighed while older warriors frowned.
Whatever their age, Ballak’s people respected the chieftain deeply, and Ahmen’s refusal looked terrible.
“I knew this old creep was hiding something!”
Aiyen stepped forward impatiently.
“Open it right now!”
Someone moved before she could.
Ahun.
Ahmen’s own grandson held out a hand toward the shaman.
Aiyen stared, surprised, while Ahun spoke in a firm voice.
“No one disobeys the chieftain. If you refuse to open that sack—”
Ahun drew an arrow and nocked it.
Ahmen’s brows shot upward.
“You little bastard!”
“….”
Ahun did not look away.
Watching Aheul almost die from the Red Death had changed something fundamental in him.
Other warriors began shouting at Ahmen as well.
“Give us the sack!”
“Show us what’s inside!”
“Traitor! You caused this!”
The accusations twisted Ahmen’s face.
Then—
“Fine! Look, then!”
He tore the sack from his belt.
And hurled it into the river beside them.
For a moment, everyone went silent.
An eerie stillness settled over the water.
Ahmen looked back at Akwilla with a forced smile.
“Oops. This old man’s hands shake. I must have dropped it.”
He tried to laugh, then stopped.
Something about the reaction around him felt wrong.
The warriors murmured and glanced toward one person.
Vikir stood nearby with his arms folded.
Akwilla looked at him and nodded.
“Exactly as you said. He threw the sack into the river.”
“…What?”
Ahmen stared at her.
Then—
“Chief! I found it!”
A head suddenly surfaced from the river.
A Ballak warrior who had been waiting underwater grabbed the discarded sack.
Even soaked, it was still partly full.
“No!”
Ahmen lunged, but the warrior had already handed it to Akwilla.
“….”
The chieftain opened the sack.
Inside was a quantity of white powder.
Akwilla turned toward Ahmen.
“This is supposed to be the antidote to the Red Death.”
“….”
“Why were you carrying it?”
Ahmen opened his mouth but produced no answer.
Akwilla nodded again.
“Just as predicted.”
She looked toward Vikir once more.
Then, while Ahmen shook his head frantically, Akwilla dumped some of the powder onto the ground.
“It’s only flour. It does nothing.”
“What!?”
“Vikir said you would believe it was an antidote. Your reaction confirms that you did.”
Ahmen stared at the powder in horror.
“That can’t be right. That has to be the antidote…”
“House Leviathan deceived you.”
Vikir stepped forward.
Leviathan had used a gullible native collaborator to spread the plague while never giving him a real cure.
They had recognized Ahmen’s hunger for power and exploited it.
The warriors’ mood darkened as one prediction after another proved accurate.
Ahmen began pleading.
“I-I don’t know! If it’s flour, then it’s just flour!”
“Then why did you refuse to show the sack, and why did you run straight to the river where the Red Death began?”
“Shut up! Why should I answer you?”
Ahmen’s voice rose, but his position only worsened.
Vikir had prepared more incriminating material.
He produced two letters.
Two different styles of handwriting covered the pages: one rough and uneven, the other much neater.
Vikir held them up.
“These are letters exchanged between you and House Leviathan.”
The evidence looked decisive.
One of the samples matched Ahmen’s handwriting closely enough to fool anyone familiar with it.
The warriors looked from the letters to the shaman.
Ahmen shouted.
“It’s fake! I never wrote that!”
No one believed him.
Several elders immediately said the handwriting looked exactly like Ahmen’s.
“Aaah! It’s a lie! He’s framing me!”
Ahmen pointed at Vikir and jumped in frustration.
Vikir only smiled faintly.
Because that accusation, at least, was true.
‘Chihuahua really was useful.’
Vikir remembered an old conversation from his time in Underdog.
‘Mr. Chihuahua, your handwriting imitation is excellent.’
‘Naturally. I’ve signed and copied enough officials’ documents that I can reproduce almost anyone’s hand.’
‘Can you teach me?’
‘Of course. It would be an honor.’
‘Good. I’ll practice whenever there’s time.’
The lessons he had taken while serving as Deputy Magistrate were paying off now.
Forgery and imitation were useful tools for propaganda and deception.
‘Agitation is easy. Explanation is hard.’
Vikir watched Ahmen pace in genuine outrage.
Meanwhile, Akwilla spoke.
“We could always investigate whether anyone in Leviathan uses the other handwriting.”
That was effectively impossible; Ballak could not simply inspect one of the Seven Great Families.
Naturally, the mood continued turning against Ahmen.
“Traitor!”
“You nearly killed my wife!”
“And my children!”
“Kill him!”
Public opinion had become vicious.
Even Ahun refused to defend his grandfather.
Ahmen ground his teeth.
In one narrow sense, he still had a point: the physical evidence was not conclusive.
If the powder was only flour and the letters could be disputed, then stubborn denial might still keep him alive.
However ugly it looked, refusing to confess was his only remaining path.
“…Enough. Everyone is getting carried away.”
Akwilla’s voice cut through the shouting.
Every warrior turned toward her, Ahmen included.
Akwilla coughed once before continuing.
“If you examine this carefully, there is no undeniable proof. Do not destroy the shaman’s honor over suspicion alone. He has served this tribe for many years.”
Murmurs spread through the crowd.
Aiyen reacted immediately.
“Mother—Chief—are you seriously going to bury this?”
“Enough! Respect the shaman’s authority. He has more years of service than you. Mind your manners.”
Aiyen fell silent under the formal rebuke.
Many warriors looked angry, but no one openly defied Akwilla.
Ahmen himself looked stunned.
Why would the chieftain, who had never liked him, suddenly defend him?
Still, it was the only escape being offered.
“I can only thank you for your wisdom and kindness.”
“It is nothing. We must honor the traditions of our ancestors.”
Ahmen nodded rapidly.
Then Akwilla’s eyes sharpened.
“How terrible that ignorant youngsters would tarnish such a respected elder’s honor.”
“Yes. Thank you, Chief.”
“Do not thank me. Your reputation is important not only in Ballak, but throughout the depths.”
“Of course…”
“The honor and pride you carry must be very heavy, Shaman.”
“…Yes?”
“To be accused without proof must have wounded that pride terribly.”
“…?”
Ahmen began to feel uneasy beneath the excessive praise.
Akwilla turned fully toward him.
“So I will give my old colleague, our spiritual teacher, the perfect chance to prove his innocence, honor, and pride. Does anyone object?”
“No!”
The warriors who understood her intent shouted together.
Vikir stepped forward calmly.
“…?”
Ahmen backed away as Vikir approached.
He still did not understand.
Akwilla’s voice hardened.
“If you are innocent, defend the reputation you have earned. After everything I have done to protect your honor, surely you will not refuse.”
“…What kind of chance?”
Anxiety flickered in Ahmen’s eyes.
Akwilla answered.
“I invoke the Illiad.”
The Illiad was Ballak’s traditional method of settling an irreconcilable dispute through force, with victory deciding whose claim prevailed.
The blood drained from Ahmen’s face.
Vikir, by contrast, smiled quietly.
Evidence and proof had never been the final objective.
Everything before this had only prepared the ground for the challenge.
