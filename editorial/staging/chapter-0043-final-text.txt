A rising star of House Morgue.
The only daughter of Raspane Morgue, head of the family.
In bloodline, talent, temperament, and appearance, she lacked nothing.
No one doubted that she would one day become the head of House Morgue.
‘And yet she is only fifteen.’
Vikir looked up beyond the wall of fire and the forest of iron skewers.
Camus Morgue stood there proudly, looking down on everyone below.
The three sisters sprawled on the ground trembled when they saw her.
“C-Camus… we’re your sisters…”
“W-We were only trying to stop intruders…”
“They started it!”
Highsis, Midsis, and Lowsis were one year older than Camus.
Yet under Camus’s overwhelming pressure, they could barely breathe.
For a prestigious magical house like Morgue, it was unusual for a mere one-year age gap to produce such an enormous difference in achievement.
Then Camus smiled.
“Camus? Did you just call me by name, sisters?”
“H-Hic! N-No, my lady!”
“Move. Unless you want to end up like that.”
Camus pointed irritably.
Burned corpses impaled on iron skewers lined the border.
There was no longer any question who had created that grisly warning.
“Eek!”
The three sisters fled in terror at the words of their one-year-younger sibling.
An eerie silence settled over the battlefield after they disappeared.
“….”
“….”
“….”
Even the Baskerville triplets, who had been baring their teeth moments ago, shrank back before Camus.
Camus then reined in her horse and rode toward the Baskerville party.
She stopped directly in front of Vikir, met his eyes, and spoke.
“Welcome, partner.”
She meant, of course, their coming joint operation against the demonic beasts and barbarian tribes.
◆◆◆
Seven years had changed Camus considerably.
The freckles were gone, as was the bucktoothed look she had worn as a child.
Some youthful softness still remained in her cheeks, but it was already obvious how striking she would become.
Vikir remembered seeing her from afar several times in his previous life.
‘She must have been around thirty then. She was beautiful.’
“Dazzling” was not too strong a word; even Vikir, who cared little for appearances, had thought so.
It was said that if all the love letters and marriage proposals sent to Camus were burned, House Morgue would have enough firewood for an entire winter.
Camus herself had made use of that attention.
In his previous life, she had kept countless powerful men dancing around her and was constantly surrounded by rumors and scandals.
It had all been strategic.
She despised men who clung to her beauty, but manipulated their feelings to provoke rivalry between houses and drew the resulting benefits toward Morgue.
Politically, she never gave any man her heart, which only made them pursue her more desperately.
She had ruled over countless captivated men.
Under her, House Morgue prospered, and she accumulated enormous achievements in the war against the demons.
…But.
That was before Vikir’s regression.
In this life, Camus was strangely free of scandal.
By fifteen, the Camus Vikir remembered should already have been entangling influential figures from the Imperial Family and the other great houses, yet now she was remarkably quiet.
There was not even a rumor that she was seeing a man.
‘I don’t understand.’
Vikir urged his horse onward, faintly puzzled.
Camus rode close beside him.
“So Baskerville’s situation is roughly this… the damage caused by the barbarian tribes has continued… and Morgue has therefore decided to form a joint front with Baskerville…”
She kept talking.
Vikir answered only when necessary, with short, dry responses.
Camus turned toward him.
“House Baskerville seems to know surprisingly little about the barbarians of Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain, doesn’t it?”
“Of course we know them. We fight them constantly. We know them better than Morgue does.”
“Then why did Baskerville send only youngsters and ordinary hounds?”
Camus had clearly noticed the low apparent quality of the delegation.
Vikir almost answered that they were decoys, then stopped himself.
Morgue already knew that Baskerville’s true main force was concealed beyond the mountain.
“Why ask when you already know?”
Camus chuckled.
“I only wanted to see whether you knew.”
“Of course I do. It concerns my family, and I am the one leading this delegation.”
“You could have been a disposable figurehead sent out by your family. But now I understand.”
Camus nudged her horse ahead of Vikir.
She glanced back with a faint smile.
“You are rather trusted within House Baskerville.”
Camus continued questioning him.
“Which barbarian tribes are the greatest problem?”
There was no way Vikir would not know; hunting and suppressing the tribes of Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain had been one of his principal duties before regression.
“From Morgue’s perspective, the Ballak warrior tribe and the Rokoko shaman tribe.”
The Ballak were among the most troublesome warrior tribes on the frontier. Their numbers were small, but every member was a formidable fighter.
“Their archery is far stronger than the Empire’s, and the principles behind it are difficult to understand.”
“Mm. Correct. They are troublesome people.”
Camus nodded, then looked at Vikir with bright eyes.
“You know the barbarian tribes quite well. Eight points.”
“Huh?”
“Out of one hundred. You failed.”
Camus stuck out her tongue, and Vikir frowned slightly.
“I answered correctly. Why is the score so low?”
“Because you answered correctly.”
“…?”
When Vikir still looked confused, Camus gave him a sly smile.
“What good is a man being so clever?”
“…?”
“I do not like clever men. Men should be a little foolish.”
The attitude that had ensnared so many men in Vikir’s previous life seemed unchanged.
Vikir urged his horse faster, deciding she did not deserve an answer.
Camus followed closely and continued grading him.
“Horsemanship: six. Maybe it’s because you’re shorter than me, but that horse looks too big for you.”
“….”
“Fashion: four. Your clothes are too dull.”
“….”
“No answer? Manners: one.”
“….”
“Face: ninety-nine. You grew up well. One point off because your expressions are terrible.”
Vikir’s ears were beginning to ache from listening to her.
He cut her off.
“Stop giving me pointless scores.”
“Why are they pointless?”
“Then what are they for?”
“Our future, obviously.”
Our future?
At Vikir’s incredulous look, Camus shrugged and puffed out her chest.
“You are a candidate to become my future husband. Of course I have to evaluate you.”
“….”
“If that seems unfair, you may evaluate me too.”
“….”
“Actually, I would rather you did. I want to know what you think of me.”
Vikir asked in disbelief.
“Why am I suddenly your husband candidate?”
“Why? You passed my uncle’s test.”
Vikir thought back to his contest with Adolf Morgue.
Adolf had balanced a water jar on his head, and Vikir had broken his own sword and used a flying fragment to shatter the jar, thereby passing Adolf’s test.
‘…That was seven years ago.’
Camus, however, spoke as though it had happened yesterday.
She flushed and burst out,
“How could Uncle decide my future husband with such a crude test! He made that promise in front of everyone, so now my marriage prospects are tied to it! But a promise is a promise. I have no choice but to accept it. I’ll accept it, I’ll accept it…!”
No one had accused her of anything, yet she was getting hotter and hotter all by herself.
Vikir watched her and thought,
‘She really is an accomplished fire mage.’
If fire magic were mastered far enough, could a person spontaneously heat up like that?
Vikir found himself mildly curious.
Still, that was one matter and this was another.
There was no benefit in antagonizing the girl who was expected to become the next head of House Morgue, so Vikir tried to be considerate.
“Forget what happened that day. I’ll pretend it never happened.”
Camus froze.
Vikir watched her.
‘A paralysis spell? Impressive. But why cast it on herself?’
Mages occasionally did things that were difficult to understand.
He did not particularly care, but diplomacy suggested that he should at least ask what was wrong.
Vikir had just opened his mouth.
“How can you pretend something never happened when it clearly did!”
Camus suddenly shrieked.
For the first time since his regression, Vikir was genuinely taken aback.
He had barely opened his mouth again when she shouted,
“I am a genius. I never forget anything I have seen!”
Something flew from Camus’s hand and struck Vikir in the face.
It was a torn piece of cloth: a small Baskerville cloak sized for an eight-year-old child.
The Baskerville crest was still clearly visible on it; it was the same cloak Vikir had once used to cover Camus.
Seven years had passed, yet it still carried a faint trace of that day.
Vikir caught it and frowned toward Camus as she rode away.
“…You kept this for seven years without washing it?”
