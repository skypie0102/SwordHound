The jungle shook under wind and rain.
The hide walls of the barracks snapped and fluttered.
An open fire burned inside.
Hot, uneven breathing came from a bed glowing red in the firelight.
Aheul, now fourteen, lay there groaning.
Red Death spots were spreading across her pale skin.
Her brother Ahun clung desperately to Vikir.
“Please, Vikir. Save my sister.”
“….”
Vikir said nothing and approached Aheul.
Red spots, violent digestive symptoms, fever, joint pain, and swollen lymph nodes all matched the Red Death.
There was no doubt about the diagnosis.
“How did she get exposed?”
“She went into the swamp to cut timber for the flood-control work and stopped there…”
Ahun answered for her.
Aheul had most likely been infected during that work in the deep wetland.
“What do we do, slave?”
Aiyen looked genuinely worried.
“Once someone is already sick, there is very little I can do here.”
Prevention could be taught to anyone; treatment belonged to people with specialized healing knowledge.
Then—
Flutter.
The entrance covering opened.
Shaman Ahmen stepped inside.
The moment he saw Aheul and Ahun, his expression hardened.
“You fools. This is what happens when you refuse to listen to your grandfather!”
Ahmen shouted directly over the sick girl.
“You followed that Imperial outsider instead of me. Now you are paying for it!”
“Grandfather, that is cruel!”
Ahun rose to protest.
Smack.
Ahmen slapped him hard.
Ahun dropped to his knees, cheek reddened, and could not immediately stand again.
Ahmen looked down at him with contempt.
“Just like your mother. Useless.”
“….”
Tears spilled from Ahun’s eyes.
Aiyen sighed with an expression that suggested this was not new behavior.
“There is a cure.”
Vikir spoke.
Ahun’s head snapped up.
Ahmen shoved past Vikir and stormed toward the exit.
“This is divine punishment. Only sacrifice can repair the offense. Now even the chieftain will have to admit it.”
The shaman clung to his authority to the very end.
Wind and rain rushed through the open entrance.
Cold droplets sprayed everyone inside.
Only Aiyen, Vikir, Ahun, and the sick Aheul remained.
Vikir turned to Ahun.
“First, collect Aheul’s waste without touching it. Boil every utensil she has used, and burn wormwood to drive away mosquitoes, fleas, bats, and other possible carriers.”
“Understood. Is that all?”
“No.”
Vikir looked at Aiyen.
Then he stated the request he had already been planning to make.
“Take me out of the depths.”
Aiyen’s expression froze.
Ballak normally made no distinction between people born inside the tribe and outsiders who had truly become members.
Within Ballak territory, accepted members could travel freely and enter nearly anywhere except the chieftain’s private barracks and restricted shamanic rites.
But there was one major restriction.
Leaving the depths entirely was forbidden.
An outsider accepted into Ballak could leave only after satisfying strict conditions.
The first condition was time.
They had to have lived with Ballak for at least two years.
The second condition concerned permanent family ties to the tribe.
Tradition expected the outsider to have a native Ballak household and at least three children before being permitted to leave freely.
Vikir did not satisfy the full customary requirements.
Even so, he asked for permission to cross beyond Ballak territory.
“If you let me leave, I will bring back medicine for the Red Death.”
Aiyen bit her lip.
Would Akwilla approve such an exception? Probably not. The chieftain was rigid about tribal law.
‘She would probably choose the tribe over one person.’
One person could be sacrificed for the greater good.
Aiyen could not accept that answer for Aheul.
She had known the girl as someone who sang, helped around the village, and worked beside everyone else.
But an even deeper fear troubled Aiyen.
Would Vikir come back?
He had originally entered Ballak as a foreign captive.
If she gave him permission to leave the depths, nothing physically guaranteed his return.
Until now, Vikir’s freedom had still ended at Ballak’s territorial boundary.
If he tried to disappear inside the jungle, Ballak trackers and wolves could find him quickly.
Vikir knew that perfectly well.
Official permission to cross the outer boundary changed everything.
If he chose not to return, he could be beyond Ballak’s reach before anyone realized it.
“….”
Aiyen hesitated.
Hesitation itself was unusual for her.
In that brief silence, she examined the reason she could not answer immediately.
She was forced to recognize an attachment she had never named.
Finally she raised her head.
Her eyes were red and wet.
“…Go.”
The order was given.
◆◆◆
Aiyen did not report the decision to Akwilla because she knew the chieftain would refuse.
She accepted responsibility herself.
Vikir ran through the flooded night with Pomeranian secured on his back.
“Flood control alone cannot stop the epidemic completely.”
Other tribes did not have Ballak’s elevated settlement or drainage works.
A more fundamental solution was necessary if the Red Death was to be stopped before it devastated the tribes, destabilized the Empire, and strengthened Baskerville through the resulting chaos.
Tap!
Vikir crossed a swollen river by stepping from one floating log to another.
Flood debris let him cut distance by running directly across the current.
Then—
He stopped sharply on the far bank.
Shhhhh.
Rain poured through the darkness while another shadow crossed the water.
Vikir turned and found Aiyen behind him, soaked and breathing hard.
“Why are you following me?”
Vikir asked. Aiyen opened her mouth, then checked herself.
“I am following my slave.”
“Do not follow me.”
“I do not want to stop.”
“I said do not follow me.”
“I am the master!”
Aiyen shouted, frustration breaking through her voice.
Vikir met her with a cold stare and drew the boundary again.
“I have told you three times. You understand what that means.”
“….”
“Do not follow me.”
The look in his eyes stopped Aiyen where she stood.
“How can you look at me like that?”
Her voice broke.
Vikir did not answer.
Aiyen searched desperately for another argument.
After several failed attempts, she finally asked the simplest question.
“Where are you going?”
“….”
“If you tell me, I will stop.”
“No.”
“Why are you taking Pomeranian?”
“….”
“Can you at least leave her with me?”
At that point, it was difficult to tell which of them was supposed to be master and which slave.
But Aiyen had understood the imbalance from the beginning.
She had sensed it even when she first saw Vikir gaunt and chained at the slave auction.
Vikir had never truly belonged beneath anyone’s authority.
Standing in the rain, Aiyen finally accepted what that meant.
She trembled before speaking again.
“Then answer one question.”
“…What?”
Vikir waited.
“You are coming back, right?”
“….”
Her voice carried naked anxiety.
This time Vikir answered immediately.
“Of course.”
Only then did Aiyen’s face soften.
She let out a long breath of relief.
“You keep your promises.”
“….”
“I will hold you to it.”
Vikir nodded.
Then—
The hound ran on into the approaching dawn.
Darkness swallowed his figure.
Aiyen remained where she was, watching until he disappeared.
