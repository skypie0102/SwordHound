—Vikir Special Law, Article 1, Section 1—
—Deputy Magistrate Vikir Van Baskerville shall immediately pay one hundred million gold to the person who pulls out this wooden stake, hereafter referred to as ‘the stake.’—
It was the first law created and enforced by the fifteen-year-old Deputy Magistrate after taking office.
No one in the legislative administration objected to its passage.
They did not object because no one understood what the law was for or what Vikir intended to accomplish with it.
The citizens stared in disbelief.
“What? He’s really giving away one hundred million?”
“You have to be joking. That’s around four years of wages for an ordinary person.”
“One hundred million just for pulling out a wooden stake?”
“What is this supposed to be, some kind of show?”
People whispered while studying Vikir, but no one could read his intention.
Chihuahua Baskerville and the other city officials were just as confused.
The whispers grew louder, yet no one stepped forward, so Vikir spoke again.
“No one?”
No one answered.
Vikir picked up his pen and added another clause to the notice.
—Vikir Special Law, Article 1, Section 2—
—Deputy Magistrate Vikir Van Baskerville shall pay an additional nine hundred million gold to the person who pulls out the stake.—
One hundred million plus another nine hundred million.
“Whoever pulls it out will now receive one billion gold in cash.”
One billion gold was more money than most ordinary people could hope to touch in an entire lifetime.
The crowd began laughing.
“This is ridiculous.”
“Is he making fun of us? Who would actually step forward for this?”
“Kid, quit playing games and go home!”
“It’s funny, though. Is this some kind of street comedy?”
The people gathered in the square openly laughed at the absurd spectacle.
Some even called Vikir a clown to his face.
Vikir remained completely calm.
“Anyone?”
A few mocking voices answered from the crowd.
Chihuahua and the other administrative officials turned red with embarrassment.
‘Why are we the ones feeling humiliated?’
The officials shifted uneasily under the citizens’ ridicule.
Vikir simply raised his pen again.
—Vikir Special Law, Article 1, Section 3—
—Deputy Magistrate Vikir Van Baskerville shall pay an additional nine billion gold to the person who pulls out the stake.—
The total reward was now ten billion gold.
The instant that number was announced, the entire square fell silent.
Absolute silence.
“Whoever pulls out this stake will receive ten billion gold in cash.”
Vikir formally proclaimed the third clause of his special law.
All sneers and taunts vanished; the central square became so quiet it seemed empty of life.
The lack of sound from such an enormous crowd was unsettling.
Even Chihuahua and the city officials were briefly overwhelmed by the scale of the silence.
Vikir remained unchanged.
“Anyone?”
This time no one answered.
The citizens looked at one another, suddenly too nervous even to laugh.
“W-what is this? What’s really going on?”
“Is he seriously offering ten billion? Should someone try?”
“Are you insane? You don’t know what happens if you step out there.”
“Look at the sword on his waist. What if he decides the volunteer insulted him and executes them on the spot?”
No one knew.
Why was a newly appointed fifteen-year-old Deputy Magistrate doing this?
No one could understand why he would apparently burn ten billion gold on a stake that anyone could pull free.
“Maybe the stake is impossibly heavy and ordinary people can’t move it?”
“No. We watched government workers carry it here without trouble.”
“Don’t go out there. There has to be another trick.”
“At times like this, the safest thing is not to stand out.”
The square stayed quiet except for occasional nervous whispers.
“….”
Vikir lifted his pen once more.
He was about to add a fourth clause.
“I-I’ll try!”
A small hand rose from the crowd.
A girl in worn clothes stepped forward carrying a basket of wilted flowers.
She walked with a limp, and her spotted, hollow face showed clear signs of malnutrition.
As she raised her hand and moved toward the open square, a kindly old man beside her tried to stop her.
He appeared to be a neighbor.
“Judy! Judy, is that you? Do you even understand where you’re going?”
“Grandpa, I’ll be okay. My mother is sick, and I have to do something. Those thugs have made it hard to sell flowers lately…”
“You foolish child! If you step forward at a time like this, you could lose your head! The world isn’t as simple as you think!”
“Hehe… If something happens to me, please look after my mother.”
Judy slipped past the old neighbor and entered the square.
Vikir looked down at her with an unreadable expression.
“Pull it out.”
Judy lowered her head and gathered her courage.
The citizens watched the frail girl with uneasy expressions.
Some pitied her, some mocked her, and others simply wanted to see what would happen next.
Judy herself broke into a cold sweat.
‘They won’t really kill me, will they? I’ve already made a fool of myself in front of everyone… maybe they’ll at least give me something.’
How much was public humiliation worth?
‘Enough to buy a basket of flowers would be fine.’
Then she could buy a hot bowl of meat porridge for her sick mother.
Under Vikir’s cold gaze, Judy stretched out her hand.
Her thin, trembling fingers closed around the stake.
Then—
Pull—!
The stake came out.
It was so light that even a malnourished girl who had barely eaten could remove it without difficulty.
“….”
Judy stared at the stake in disbelief.
The crowd and the administrative officials were equally stunned.
“She pulled it.”
“It came out.”
“She really pulled it.”
“She did it.”
The whispers spread rapidly.
Now everyone wanted to know what would happen to the girl who had actually taken Vikir at his word.
That question held the entire square in suspense.
Then—
“….”
Vikir raised one hand.
His hand moved toward his waist, where his sword hung.
Seeing the motion, Judy squeezed her eyes shut.
‘I’m going to be executed.’
She did not know what crime would be named—insulting a noble, insolence, greed, or some other offense invented after the fact.
For one brief moment, Judy thought of everyone she cared about. If she died here, who would look after her mother?
Then—
“No!”
A desperate scream cut through the square.
A painfully thin woman forced her way through the crowd.
Her clothes were rags and her body looked little more than skin and bone, yet she somehow found the strength to pull Judy into her arms and kneel before Vikir.
“My lord, please! Have mercy!”
Judy’s mother fell at Vikir’s feet with her daughter and began to beg.
“This lowly person dared to misunderstand the intentions of someone above us. Please forgive us just once! She is a good child—after her father died, she cared for her sick mother and sold flowers on the street every day even while going hungry…”
The woman began sobbing uncontrollably.
“Please show mercy. People like us are not even worthy of staining your sword with our blood. Please overlook this one offense…”
She pressed her forehead to the ground and begged for her daughter’s life.
Her cries began to anger the crowd.
“She’s right! What did Judy do wrong?”
“There isn’t a more hardworking or kinder girl in this market!”
“What crime did that mother and daughter commit?”
“Does having power mean you can torment Judy whenever you like?”
The atmosphere in the square turned dangerous.
The crowd that had been silent moments ago began to boil with anger.
Chihuahua and the other officials shifted nervously as the situation approached a riot.
Then—
Vikir reached toward Judy.
His hand passed over the hilt of his sword and closed around the thick leather pouch hanging behind it.
Thump.
Vikir held the heavy leather sack out in front of Judy.
Judy stared from the bag to Vikir’s face in confusion.
With shaking hands, she accepted it.
She pulled open the straps of the unexpectedly heavy pouch.
FWOOSH!
A brilliant golden light spilled from inside.
Gold coins—more than Judy could count.
The square fell silent again.
Judy, her mother, and every person watching them froze.
Vikir spoke.
“Ten billion gold.”
The words sounded almost unreal.
Judy and her mother remained kneeling before a fortune they had never seen, heard of, or imagined possessing.
The entire crowd was just as stunned.
“This is how the law works.”
Then Vikir addressed every citizen filling the square.
“It will be upheld.”
