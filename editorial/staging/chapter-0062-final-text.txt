Vikir sat inside his shelter, tending the small fire pit.
“…Are you there?”
A quiet voice came from outside.
Vikir stepped through the leaf-covered entrance and found a black-haired, dark-eyed girl waiting shyly.
He recognized her as one of the women who had lined up outside Akwilla’s tent the previous morning.
“The chieftain is asking for you. I came to deliver the message.”
So she had been sent as a messenger.
Vikir immediately started toward the chieftain’s tent.
Then—
“Ah, wait!”
The girl called after him.
When Vikir looked back, she had gone red and was stumbling over her words.
“Thank you for smoking the meat yesterday. It was really good.”
She meant the oxbear meat from the hunting festival.
Vikir gave a brief nod and continued on; if the chieftain had summoned him, he should not keep her waiting.
◆◆◆
Even Ballak warriors who roamed the jungle freely showed strict restraint in the presence of Chieftain Akwilla.
Vikir passed through the tent curtain and immediately saw the great eagle carving mounted at the far end.
Beneath it, Akwilla sat in a large chair.
Her expression was stern, and scars crossed her face.
She was not especially tall, but the eagle-feather cloak and sheer pressure around her made her seem enormous.
The broad scar crossing one eye made her presence even more intimidating.
Known as the Night Fox, Akwilla was Ballak’s current chieftain and strongest warrior.
She had clashed successfully with both Hugo Le Baskerville, Swordmaster and head of Baskerville, and Adolf Morgue, Morgue’s sixth-circle deputy.
Her existence was a major reason a tribe of barely three hundred could make the Empire fear the Black Mountain frontier.
“Chieftain.”
Vikir dropped to one knee and bowed as his old training demanded.
Akwilla was considered the greatest Ballak archer since Adonai, the legendary warrior who had led the tribe centuries earlier.
Her remaining eye studied Vikir with open interest.
Ballak speech differed sharply from Imperial etiquette: it went directly to the point instead of circling through layers of greeting and praise.
“I heard you helped bring down an oxbear. Thanks to you, the village ate well.”
Akwilla smiled, unexpectedly gentle.
Her voice carried authority comparable to Hugo’s, but without his cutting edge.
“No, Chieftain. I only did what was necessary.”
“No false modesty. A deed well done should be recognized.”
Akwilla’s smile deepened.
Vikir was genuinely surprised to see such warmth from the feared Night Fox.
Then Akwilla gave her decision.
“For your contribution to the hunt, I release you from bondage. From today onward, you are not a slave. You are one of Ballak.”
With those words, Vikir’s status formally changed from captive slave to recognized tribe member.
A moment later—
“…Cough.”
A dry, openly displeased cough sounded nearby.
Vikir glanced sideways and saw an elderly man glaring at him.
‘The old shaman who blackens the hunters’ faces before they leave.’
His name was Ahmen, Ballak’s senior shaman.
Within the village, few people besides Akwilla held more institutional authority.
Young hunters disliked his old rituals and superstitions, but many older veterans trusted him deeply.
There really were few atheists in a foxhole.
People who had survived enough hardship often wanted something spiritual to rely on, and Ahmen understood that need.
‘And Ahun is his grandson—or at least his direct family.’
Ahun’s open resentment suddenly made more sense when Vikir saw the same displeasure on the old shaman’s face.
Ahmen plainly disliked Akwilla’s decision to accept an Imperial outsider.
Akwilla noticed his expression as easily as Vikir did.
“Shaman.”
She turned toward Ahmen.
“…Yes, Chieftain.”
“You object to my accepting this boy into the tribe?”
Akwilla asked without softening the question.
Ahmen carried considerable influence, but the chieftain’s authority remained absolute.
He immediately bowed his head rather than challenge her directly.
“Of course not. I am only concerned about introducing Imperial blood into the tribe.”
“That is not your decision. Your duty is to read the rains, bless the hunters, tend the sacred fires in war, oversee funerals, and maintain wards against plague.”
At Akwilla’s blunt answer, Ahmen lowered his head further.
Across the tent, Aiyen clicked her tongue quietly.
Whatever the shaman thought, the matter was settled.
Akwilla had recognized Vikir’s hunt contribution and made him an official tribe member.
Her gaze shifted to his still-limping body.
“You have recovered a surprising amount already.”
“I owe that to the tribe’s care.”
“Not mine. If anyone deserves credit, it is my daughter.”
Akwilla glanced toward Aiyen by the wall.
“….”
Aiyen avoided her mother’s eyes and muttered something under her breath.
Akwilla returned her attention to Vikir.
“Now that you are one of Ballak, name a reward. I did not raise you from birth as I did my own people, so let me give you something worthy of your contribution.”
She considered the possibilities.
“There are many people your age in the village. If you wanted a formal partner, I could arrange an introduction. Or if you need workers, captured outsiders can sometimes be assigned as dependents.”
A brief murmur sounded beyond the tent.
It came from the same young villagers who had been waiting outside Akwilla’s tent since dawn.
Vikir shook his head.
“I appreciate the offer, but I do not need a partner or attendant.”
Ballak used family ties and household obligations as practical ways to integrate outsiders permanently into the tribe.
Akwilla considered his refusal.
“Hmm. You want neither? That is unusual. Most outsiders ask for family, wealth, or status first.”
The Ballak were, as a population, unusually healthy-looking.
Constant hunting and training kept them physically strong, and their hair and eyes showed little of the malnutrition common in poorer tribes.
Compared with many neighboring peoples, Ballak warriors stood out sharply.
Regular introduction of outsiders also helped the small population avoid hereditary problems from isolation.
That health and reputation had made Ballak captives desirable targets for slavers in the past.
Most raiders who tried, however, ended up dead under Ballak arrows or fed to the wolves.
Akwilla wrinkled her nose thoughtfully.
“Then what about half of the oxbear hide you brought back?”
A complete oxbear hide was an extremely valuable material.
Vikir almost reacted visibly at the suggestion of cutting it in half.
Dividing a hide destroyed most of its value; anyone who understood materials would avoid doing that unless necessary.
Just then—
“…!”
Vikir felt a sharp stare from one side.
Aiyen was glaring meaningfully at him.
‘You did not forget what I told you, did you?’
Her expression communicated the reminder without words.
Akwilla glanced sideways at her daughter, apparently unused to seeing Aiyen signal so desperately.
Aiyen kept trying to catch Vikir’s eye, unaware that her mother had already noticed everything.
At last Vikir made the request Aiyen had instructed him to make.
“I do not need a material reward. If possible, I would like permission to enter the Fountain of Valor.”
Before the summons, Aiyen had insisted that this should be his chosen reward.
For the first time during the audience, Akwilla frowned.
“…Hmm.”
If even the otherwise favorable chieftain hesitated, the elders’ response was predictable.
Several older warriors immediately swallowed angry objections.
Ahmen, standing nearest the front, finally snapped.
“Impudent boy! Do you understand what you are asking?”
The shaman strode forward and knelt before Akwilla.
“Chieftain! For generations, only Ballak warriors who proved exceptional valor have been allowed to enter that spring.”
Murmurs spread through the tent.
Ahun, standing near the entrance, added a bitter remark.
“The Fountain of Valor is for those who have truly proven themselves. One hunt does not make him worthy.”
Aiyen answered from the opposite side.
“Vikir has proven himself. He helped take an oxbear when the tribe desperately needed meat.”
“Hmph. He probably hid behind you and threw pebbles.”
“And did a pebble leave your back wrapped in bandages?”
Ahun stiffened when Aiyen pointed toward his still-supported waist.
At Akwilla’s questioning look, Vikir waited quietly.
“The Fountain of Valor… you know what it is?”
Deep within Ballak’s sacred grounds, beneath the Tomb of Champions, lay a small spring reserved for the tribe’s greatest warriors.
Tradition held that ancestral blessing in the water accelerated recovery from internal injuries and deep physical trauma.
But the spring refilled very slowly, and its effect weakened when too many people used it, so access had always been tightly restricted.
“Chieftain, this is too much. How can someone who arrived only recently enter the tribe’s holiest ground?”
“He brought down an oxbear when food was scarce. Rewarding potential once is not unreasonable.”
“Nonsense. Since when is the Fountain of Valor an ordinary bath?”
“He is young, injured, and now one of our own. The tribe should not ignore that either.”
The elders began arguing among themselves.
Some valued the oxbear hunt highly because the village had genuinely needed meat; others considered one achievement insufficient.
Akwilla herself appeared inclined to grant the request, but the oxbear alone gave her limited political ground against tradition.
Ahmen and his faction already disliked Vikir’s admission into the tribe and opposed opening the Fountain even more strongly.
Akwilla wanted to honor Aiyen’s request if possible, yet overruling the shaman and conservative elders outright would create needless conflict.
Then—
Clang, clang, clang!
The alarm bell atop the watchtower at the edge of the settlement suddenly rang.
A sentry shouted from above.
“Outsiders are approaching!”
Something had happened at the village boundary.
