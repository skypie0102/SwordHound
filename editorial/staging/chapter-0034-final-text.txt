The rumors spread quickly.
The seven heirs who had lived in arrogance, luxury, and vice had all been arrested at once.
Whenever three or more Underdog citizens gathered, the arrests became a topic of conversation.
“They tried to obstruct the new Deputy Magistrate and finally met someone who wouldn’t tolerate it.”
“Those are the same bastards who treated commoners like insects. Looks like they finally found the wrong person.”
“But what happens to them now? The new Deputy Magistrate doesn’t seem normal enough to simply release them.”
“They’re still heirs of powerful local families. He’ll probably humiliate them and then let them go.”
As they spoke, people naturally turned toward the north side of the central square.
The old legal notice still fluttered there in the wind.

…
The notice was unchanged from when it had first been posted.
The law contained no exception for status.
There was no clause allowing the rich or powerful to buy forgiveness.
The seven heirs’ crimes were hardly ambiguous.
They had participated in illegal brothels, illegal lobbying, human trafficking, drug consumption and distribution, and the accumulation of illicit profits.
Tax evasion, assault, and sexual violence were additional charges.
The ultimate sentence was therefore clear.
Death.
No excuse or political consideration erased that penalty.
People whispered.
“Is he really going to execute the sons of those seven local families?”
“Don’t you know the new Deputy Magistrate? He paid ten billion over one wooden stake.”
“Even so, killing all seven will cause enormous repercussions.”
“He’ll probably beat them, humiliate them, and stop there.”
“Right. I doubt he’ll actually carry out a death sentence.”
Nearly everyone reached the same conclusion.
This was supposedly a power contest between a new administrator and entrenched local magnates; if the old families bowed deeply enough, politics would smooth everything over.
As expected, Houses Montblanc, Pierre, Louis Vuitton, Chanel, Ferragamo, Hermès, and Prada sent formal apologies to the new Deputy Magistrate.
The local magnates lowered their heads.
Citizens clicked their tongues when family carriages loaded with extravagant tribute rolled toward City Hall in the middle of the night.
Surely the seven detained heirs would now be released alive and receive only a severe reprimand from their fathers.
That would be the end of it.
Perhaps the families would seek revenge later, perhaps they would temporarily bow and look away, and perhaps the seven heirs would behave for a while.
The young Deputy Magistrate would have won a respectable political victory over the local elite.
Citizens would feel moderately satisfied, moderately angry, and then gradually forget the obvious compromise.
That was how such things worked.
…
…Or so everyone assumed.
Until that morning, when seven severed heads appeared in the middle of the central square.
Seven heads, preserved with salt for public display.
Without their bodies, the dead men’s faces remained twisted by the agony they had endured before execution.
Notices beneath the heads listed the additional statutory punishments imposed before death.

…
…
The citizens could only stare.
The statutes themselves had always existed, so no one could claim Vikir had invented harsher penalties for these particular prisoners.
What shocked them was the status of the people to whom the law had actually been applied.
This was no longer a simple political contest.
It had become an exterminating struggle that would continue until one side was broken.
Everyone now expected bloodshed.
◆◆◆
Not long before the executions—
Vikir stood before the bars of the City Hall dungeon.
Inside, the seven battered heirs lay imprisoned, bloodied and groaning.
“You shameless bastard! Our fathers know everything! They’ll kill you!”
“Get out of here, Baskerville! How dare you do this to us!”
“Please, my lord! Overlook this and let us go!”
Chihuahua Baskerville stood nearby, visibly restless.
“Deputy Magistrate… what did you do to beat them into this state?”
Despite his words, Chihuahua looked more satisfied than horrified.
Vikir turned his expressionless face toward him.
“Do you think I was too harsh?”
“What? Of course! They deserved a beating, but if you turn them into meat like this, how are we supposed to deal with the aftermath?”
Vikir gave a faint smile.
“Don’t worry. I haven’t even started.”
“…?”
Chihuahua stared at him in confusion.
Vikir did not answer immediately.
Local self-governing councils had originally existed to watch local maladministration, educate and organize communities, and coordinate relations with City Hall.
Over time, however, the institutions had rotted until influential local families used them to challenge even Baskerville’s authority.
“You’re finished when our families come for you!”
“Just wait!”
“You bastard Baskerville dog!”
Their furious howling only confirmed how entitled they had become.
Vikir absorbed their hatred without reacting.
“I will execute all seven of you at sunset.”
…!
The prisoners fell silent at once.
Vikir continued.
“And while I’m at it, I’ll wipe out every accomplice—from the vermin in that club to the criminals infesting the streets.”
There was no bluff in his voice.
Vikir had already shown that once he declared something, he followed through.
A war on crime had been declared.
The heirs rushed to the bars and shook them desperately.
“Please! Spare us!”
“Let us out! Please!”
“We’ll give you everything our families own!”
People who normally treated others like insects now crawled and begged like insects themselves.
Chihuahua watched with complicated emotions.
He wondered whether he would ever witness anything like this again.
At the same time, he feared retaliation from the local magnates.
Their retaliation would be quiet but potentially devastating.
Their networks could boycott Vikir’s administration, refuse cooperation with city events, and isolate official business.
Trade with neighboring cities could suffer, and tax revenue could shrink.
Vikir remained unconcerned.
He looked as though he had already calculated every consequence.
“There is nothing to worry about.”
“…But, sir—”
“Their families will not be in a position to protest.”
“What? Why?”
Vikir answered Chihuahua with unusual ease.
“They are connected to criminal organizations throughout Underdog, large and small.”
“What? H-how do you know that…?”
Vikir remained silent rather than admit that he had seen those connections before his regression.
Chihuahua’s worry only deepened.
“But evidence is what matters. Even if we find some, what stops those families from retaliating afterward?”
“Don’t worry. It is all under control.”
Vikir smiled.
Evidence could be found. If necessary, evidence could also be fabricated.
What happened much later was not his immediate concern.
Vikir was already planning to move on.
‘I don’t know who will replace me, but they are going to have a difficult job.’
Vikir did not expect to remain Deputy Magistrate for very long.
The world would soon change violently, and there was a good chance Hugo would send him into real combat before much more time passed.
Making enemies here therefore posed little threat to Vikir’s long-term plans.
If he secured Academy admission, he would leave Underdog immediately anyway.
“So while I am Deputy Magistrate, I may as well discipline them thoroughly.”
His solution was to prescribe powerful medicine even if it produced serious side effects.
The results would soon become part of the reputation accelerating Vikir’s career.
Whoever inherited the post afterward was not his concern; Vikir had never been fond of Baskerville’s own ruling class either.
…Still, a journey of a thousand miles begins with one step.
First, he needed these seven heirs to reveal every connection they had to criminal organizations, along with the locations of those groups.
Vikir stepped closer to the bars.
“I’ll tell you the charges against you.”
“….”
“Illegal kidnapping and imprisonment of women, sexual assault, intimidation, forced administration of illegal drugs, human trafficking, coercion into prostitution, bribery of officials, extortion, blackmail, commissioned violence, and even murder…”
The seven erupted in protest.
“That’s a false accusation! Punish us when you have evidence!”
“So you admit there are crimes, but claim I have no proof?”
“T-that’s…”
The heirs exchanged uneasy looks.
Then determination hardened their faces.
“Even if we die here, we won’t bring ruin on our families.”
If they confessed, they could no longer expect their families to avenge them.
Their houses might abandon them, and a man like Vikir might use the confession to extend collective punishment through the entire family network.
‘Never. We won’t say a word.’
The seven resolved to remain silent even if it killed them.
…But—
“Oh. You’re finally here.”
Vikir raised one hand toward the dungeon corridor.
Apparently, he had been waiting in front of the cell for someone.
“…?”
The prisoners followed his gaze with growing anxiety.
A short, gaunt old man approached carrying a large basket.
“Your custom order is ready, my lord.”
He was an interrogation specialist who worked beneath City Hall.
Then—
Vikir overturned the basket and emptied its contents onto a shelf.
Clatter—clatter.
More than a hundred knives spilled out.
They were grotesquely shaped—bent, twisted, distorted, blunt in some places and viciously sharp in others.
The old interrogator grinned through yellowed teeth.
“I’ve interrogated people for thirty years and never seen tools like these. How did you invent something this disturbing?”
“I didn’t invent them. Where I came from, devices like these were common.”
“Was the place you came from hell?”
“Close enough. You may learn something useful.”
Even a thirty-year interrogation veteran looked unsettled by the instruments.
Vikir gathered them and walked back toward the cell.
“…!”
The seven heirs instinctively crawled away from the bars.
But there was nowhere to go.
CLANG.
Vikir opened the cell door without hesitation and stepped inside.
He remembered the faces of old comrades from before his regression.
‘How nostalgic. Under these methods, even captured demons gave up military secrets.’
Interrogation techniques from the Age of Destruction had been brutal enough to break creatures that came from hell itself.
To Vikir, who remembered how rapidly those techniques had evolved during the demon war, the heirs’ determined expressions almost looked innocent.
“You’re all making faces as though you’ll never talk.”
Vikir gave them the faintest smile.
‘…Try not to break too quickly.’
He meant it.
He wanted to savor the grim nostalgia of the past a little longer.
