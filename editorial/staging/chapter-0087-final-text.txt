“The Illiad?”
Ahmen’s expression hardened.
“The Illiad is a contest between warriors—a final struggle for honor from which neither side can retreat.”
Akwilla smiled calmly at the shaman.
“Ahmen, great shaman of Ballak, you are a man of pride, honor, and tradition. Surely you will not let those things be stained.”
Ahmen ground his teeth.
Only now did he understand why the chieftain who had always disliked him had spent so much time praising him.
Every word had been preparation designed to make refusing the Illiad impossible.
Akwilla intended to use this chance to break the political power the shaman faction had accumulated around the chieftaincy.
For years she had encouraged Aiyen and the younger generation to question superstition and outdated customs.
Even Ahmen’s own grandson refused to support him.
“Grandfather. You always said tradition must be honored. The Illiad is Ballak tradition.”
“Shut up! I know!”
Ahmen turned away with clenched teeth.
Vikir stood in front of him without expression.
The outsider who once entered Ballak as a captive had become a tribal hero.
In less than two years, Vikir had gained extraordinary recognition.
Ahmen had spent a lifetime chasing that same recognition.
The comparison made Ahmen furious.
His hatred flared like fire over an oil field.
“Fine! I accept! I challenge you to the Illiad!”
Vikir answered only with a silent nod.
Acceptance.
Akwilla leaned toward Vikir and spoke so quietly only he could hear.
“Can you win?”
“If you ask me to do it, I’ll do it.”
Before and after his regression, Vikir had spent decades as Baskerville’s hound.
Being used as someone else’s blade was familiar territory.
Akwilla smiled in satisfaction.
Having Vikir on her side felt like holding a perfectly sharpened weapon.
“Very well. I believe you, son-in-law.”
“…?”
For a moment, Vikir was reminded uncomfortably of Adolf Morgue.
The warriors cleared space for the confrontation.
It was a place where two people would stake their standing and lives on combat.
Everyone watched in tense silence.
Ahmen tried to reassure himself.
‘Good. I can use this to destroy that brat and reclaim everything I lost.’
He glared at Vikir.
If Vikir disappeared, Ahmen imagined his old position would naturally return.
“The date will be the full moon three days from now!”
He raised his voice.
“Three nights from now, here at this place, I formally challenge you to the Illiad!”
The surrounding warriors were struck by the intensity of his declaration.
Vikir looked completely indifferent.
Then—
“Three days? Let’s do it now.”
He spoke as though he wanted to finish an annoying chore.
The warriors murmured at his composure.
Cold sweat appeared on Ahmen’s back.
The three days he had hoped to use for secret preparation were gone.
Vikir knew exactly why Ahmen wanted time and refused to give it to him.
“That’s how shamans fight.”
Delay, preparation, hidden tools, and ritual advantages were all part of the trade.
But Vikir had spent a lifetime surviving worse schemers.
Compared with the enemies of the Age of Destruction, Ahmen’s tricks were familiar.
“The challenger chooses the date and place under Illiad tradition, correct?”
Ahmen flinched.
He had spent years preaching obedience to tradition.
Now he could hardly reject that tradition when it bound him.
His own words had become restraints.
Aiyen laughed.
“As expected of Ahmen.”
Then she glanced toward Bakira.
The wolf waited beside her as calmly as ever.
Aiyen stroked Bakira’s ears and whispered an order.
“Go tell the village to prepare a welcome for the Illiad’s winner.”
She clearly meant a celebration for Vikir.
But—
“Don’t.”
Vikir stopped her.
Aiyen narrowed her eyes.
“If you assume victory too early, the opposite result has a way of finding you.”
Certainty made people careless about variables.
Vikir watched Ahmen from a distance.
Shamans were dangerous precisely because they fought with hidden variables.
Vikir had battled shamans, warlocks, alchemists, and similar opponents throughout the future war.
Nearly all of them had kept one final surprise in reserve.
Vikir had survived those surprises, but he had learned never to ignore them.
“I don’t lower my guard even one percent.”
A good hound used its full strength even against a rabbit.
That was even more important when the prey was an old fox.
Vikir reviewed everything he remembered from his previous life.
He considered every contingency he could imagine.
His caution made Aiyen more serious as well.
Wanting to help, she began telling him everything she knew about Ahmen.
One detail made Vikir’s attention sharpen.
“Wait. Repeat that part.”
Aiyen brightened at his interest.
“Which part? The ridiculous clothing preferences the laundry boys complain about?”
“…Before that.”
“Oh. His birth and background.”
Aiyen lowered her voice and repeated a secret she had learned from Akwilla.
Only Akwilla, Aiyen, and Ahmen himself were supposed to know it.
“Ahmen wasn’t born Ballak. He came from another tribe and was taken here when he was young, probably around the age you were when you first arrived.”
Vikir was surprised.
He had assumed Ahmen’s obsession with Ballak tradition came from generations of native status.
That assumption had been wrong.
Instead, the shaman had originally been an outsider.
Aiyen’s next explanation was darker.
“His story about being brought here as an honored partner was false. He was taken as a slave and abused by the shaman who controlled him.”
Ahmen’s hostility toward Vikir may have been sharpened because he saw an echo of his own youth in the younger outsider.
That echo gave the old man another reason to resent him.
The resemblance had curdled into resentment rather than sympathy.
Vikir had followed the path Ahmen wished he had taken.
Outsider, survivor, then admired member of the tribe.
That difference likely made Ahmen’s jealousy worse.
“Eventually Ahmen killed the shaman who owned him and took his place.”
Aiyen described the brutal years that followed.
Ahmen had spent them forcing his way into Ballak society.
“He had no place in a culture that prized strength, and people also despised him for killing the shaman who raised him. He did anything he could to be accepted.”
He performed the filthiest and most dangerous work in the village.
He handled waste, drove dangerous prey, served as bait, and accepted every humiliation offered to him.
He even woke before dawn to warm the previous chieftain’s shoes with his body.
When Akwilla was still a child, Ahmen carried her around and imitated a dog simply to make her laugh.
By the time the tribe finally treated him as one of their own, most of his youth was gone.
Acceptance had come only after years of humiliation.
As an old man, he became painfully conscious of everything he believed had been denied to him.
Years of humiliation and suppressed anger turned into entitlement.
He began craving more than respect.
He wanted control over Akwilla, Ballak, and eventually the wider jungle.
That ambition was what made an alliance with Leviathan attractive.
Vikir considered the story.
“…What tribe was Ahmen born into?”
“Hm. I don’t know.”
Vikir nodded.
For the immediate duel, the answer seemed irrelevant.
Across the clearing, Ahmen had been growing more nervous by the minute.
His agitation worsened when Vikir stepped into the space prepared for the Illiad.
Vikir advanced like a seasoned hound tightening a leash one step at a time.
He had prepared for every danger he could imagine.
But before the Illiad could properly begin, something happened that even Vikir had not anticipated.
BOOM!
