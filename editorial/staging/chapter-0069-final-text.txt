Every Rokoko hunter in the camp was dead.
Vikir and Aiyen advanced through the heat and foul smell of the bodies.
“Is this…?”
Aiyen narrowed her eyes.
Even in death, the Rokoko hunters told a clear story.
Their bodies revealed how they had died.
Vomiting and diarrhea stained the ground; dying hunters had tried to warm chilled bodies with fires and leaves, and red spots covered their skin.
Aiyen swallowed.
“So even the warriors sent out to hunt ended up like this.”
Only the relatively healthy members of the tribe would have been chosen for hunting duty.
Neither of them wanted to imagine what condition the people left in the village might be in.
“Let’s go.”
Vikir took the lead.
He and Aiyen crossed directly into Rokoko territory.
Normally, Rokoko claimed a wide perimeter and marked it aggressively.
Skulls and corpses were usually hung along the approaches as warnings, but the markers here had not been renewed.
Most were old enough to be covered in moss and mold.
That neglect was completely unlike the normally territorial Rokoko.
“Something happened to the village.”
Aiyen pushed through the neglected boundary and headed toward the low hills where Rokoko lived.
There was no sign of life.
Even though it was past mealtime, no cooking smoke rose from the settlement.
At the village entrance, both of them stopped.
It was not merely that there were no guards; there was no one moving anywhere inside.
Roughly built barracks stood empty while weeds grew across their floors.
Crude household goods had been abandoned where they fell.
“Did they move somewhere else?”
Aiyen frowned and pulled aside the covering of the nearest barracks.
Then—
Bzzzzzz!
A huge swarm of flies exploded outward.
The smell of filth and rotten flesh was overwhelming.
Inside lay the bodies of three small children and a woman.
They appeared to have died only recently.
Vikir checked the neighboring barracks.
“The others are the same.”
Rokoko’s people had died inside their shelters before many of them could even leave.
Most corpses were badly decomposed, but every body with intact skin showed the same red spots.
“What is this… a plague?”
Aiyen shuddered.
People of the depths feared poisonous epidemics more than almost anything, so her reaction was understandable.
“….”
Vikir stared at the bodies in silence.
Then an old memory surfaced from before his regression.
“…The Red Death.”
The name became clearer as he remembered.
A terrifying epidemic called the Red Death had spread through the frontier around this period.
It had been a first-class plague that killed enormous numbers among the jungle tribes.
The outbreak had become severe enough to reach the borders of the Empire.
Victims became bedridden and helpless before dying slowly.
Their metabolism weakened drastically, prolonging the process.
Red spots spread over the skin, followed by vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, severe pain, collapse, and death.
The disease spread so quickly that terrified tribespeople believed even eye contact could transmit it.
“How was it cured?”
Vikir searched the distant memory for the answer.
Then—
“No, slave!”
A hand seized Vikir’s collar.
He turned and found Aiyen shouting urgently.
“We need to leave. Now!”
“Why?”
“Why? This is a curse from the gods!”
For once, Aiyen was genuinely frightened.
Vikir caught her wrist before she could drag him away.
“Calm down. It is a disease.”
“What!? That is worse! We need to leave! Rokoko must have brought this on themselves with their forbidden rites!”
“It does not spread that easily.”
Vikir looked back across the abandoned village.
Aiyen jumped at every sound but refused to leave him behind.
She stayed close, clutching his collar as she followed.
“Are you sure we are not being cursed?”
“If you are that afraid, go back first.”
“And what if you die here?”
“Then I die.”
Vikir answered flatly, and Aiyen snapped at him.
“Who cares about you dying alone?”
“…?”
Vikir understood perfectly well that she did, but did not say so.
In any case—
A quick survey showed that Rokoko had not been completely exterminated.
The surviving members had apparently fled, leaving the dead and dying behind.
“If any of the refugees are carriers, moving elsewhere may only spread the same disaster.”
Vikir could understand the desperate decision to abandon the village.
But escaping the settlement did not guarantee escape from the Red Death.
The empty village also offered a rare chance to learn more about Rokoko culture.
Vikir searched several barracks and collected books and objects that appeared important.
Someday the material could contribute to serious study of Rokoko and the other tribes of the Black Mountains.
Just then—
Aiyen screamed from outside.
“Kyaaaaa!”
The scream was urgent enough that Vikir immediately ran from the barracks.
Aiyen’s frightened shouting only grew louder.
“The curse! There is a cursed child!”
Fear of plague and divine punishment ran deep among the peoples of the depths.
Vikir followed Aiyen’s stare.
A child stood at the edge of the settlement beside the smallest, poorest barracks.
“…Imperial?”
Vikir narrowed his eyes.
The child clearly did not look Rokoko.
She appeared to be about five years old.
She had black hair, red eyes, and snow-pale skin.
Barefoot, she stood beside the collapsing barracks and refused to move away from it.
Behind the shelter stood a crude cairn of recently stacked stones.
A few freshly picked purple flowers had been laid before it.
“A ghost! A cursed child of the Forest God! We have to run, Vikir! You go first—I’ll follow! My legs won’t move!”
Aiyen trembled badly.
Vikir gave her an incredulous look after two years of seeing almost nothing frighten her.
More importantly, he needed to learn who the child was.
“She may be the last person left in this Rokoko village.”
Vikir approached and crouched so they were at eye level.
The girl flinched but did not pull away when Vikir gently touched her head.
“Who are you?”
“…?”
The girl only shook her head.
Vikir tried again several times without getting a normal answer.
Instead, she forced out a few broken words.
“Rokoko. Slave. Kitchen slave.”
Her speech mixed Rokoko and Imperial awkwardly.
Vikir reconstructed the likely meaning from context.
“So you were a slave here. What is your name?”
“…Pomeranian.”
Vikir nodded.
“Your mother gave you an Imperial name?”
Pomeranian was unusual, but still recognizably Imperial.
Whatever her history, the girl clearly came from the Empire.
Vikir moved to the next question.
He was about to ask what had happened to Rokoko.
Then the girl said something that made his entire body stiffen.
“…La Baskerville.”
Vikir froze.
Among Baskerville, the middle name “La” was reserved for direct female-line family members.
Very few people in the house were permitted the gendered middle names Les or La.
Vikir’s shock frightened the girl enough that she tried to hide behind a support post.
For a moment, no sound came from him.
Even Vikir could be stunned badly enough to lose his voice.
He wiped his face several times and forced himself to recover.
Once his expression settled, he spoke gently.
“Child, come here.”
“….”
The girl stayed behind the post and only peeked around it.
Vikir wondered how to make himself less frightening.
At that moment, Aiyen emerged from another barracks and tossed something toward him.
“Hey. I found this inside. It isn’t cursed, is it?”
Vikir caught it.
It was a small gold pendant.
A Baskerville fang emblem was embossed on the front.
Aiyen recognized the symbol from the medicine container Vikir had once given her.
“Isn’t that your family’s crest?”
“….”
The pendant made the child’s claim much harder to dismiss.
Vikir ran a finger over the Baskerville insignia.
Its design was old-fashioned, the sort of accessory that had been popular roughly thirty years earlier.
Click.
Vikir opened it.
Inside was a tiny portrait painted with extraordinary precision.
“This…?”
Vikir stared at the image.
It showed a young woman, a young man, and a girl who looked to be in her early teens.
The woman had blond hair and blue eyes; the man had the dark hair and red eyes typical of Baskerville; the girl between them had dark hair and red eyes as well.
Vikir recognized the young man immediately.
“Hugo Le Baskerville…!?”
There was no mistake: the portrait showed Hugo as a young man.
