Dolores L. Quovadis.
She was sixteen and already considered the strongest candidate to become the next head of House Quovadis.
An undisputed Colosseo Academy prodigy, she had entered early, never missed a class, and now served as student-council vice president in her second year.
Seeing her reminded Vikir of the future he remembered.
In the Age of Destruction, Dolores had traveled to the worst battlefronts, healing the wounded and showing what a living saint should be.
Soldiers called her an angel of the battlefield because she saved countless lives.
Even now, she already possessed unusual clarity and resolve for her age.
Like many survivors of the Age of Destruction, Vikir held genuine respect for the young saintess.
Vikir lowered his head, making the nearby paladins tense.
Dolores paused as well.
Her spiritual senses seemed sharp enough to notice the blood, oil, violence, anger, and hatred clinging to Vikir’s presence.
Beneath all of it, she sensed something heavier: deep, tightly buried grief.
“Are you the lost lamb?”
“…Yes.”
Vikir answered quietly.
Dolores nodded.
“You feel more like a puppy than a lamb.”
“….”
“A scarred little puppy.”
Several paladins on the first floor visibly reacted to the description.
Mozgus protested at once.
“Saintess, that is not a puppy. That is a blood-mad wolf. Please step back—”
“Bring tea.”
Dolores raised one hand and stopped Mozgus.
Her tone became firm.
“I need to speak with him.”
◆◆◆
Soon a small parlor was prepared with a table, chairs, and two cups.
Steam rose from peppermint tea.
Mozgus stood uneasily at the entrance.
He held a small floral teapot that looked almost absurd in his massive hand.
Vikir and Dolores sat across from each other at the table.
Dolores spoke first.
“When I heard Mozgus had gone out personally, I came because I thought you were going to be badly hurt.”
Mozgus had the temperament, beliefs, and physique of an inquisitor.
A suspiciously dressed intruder had given him every reason to respond aggressively.
But events had gone very differently.
The military backbone of Quovadis had been defeated with shocking speed.
Mozgus immediately objected from the doorway.
“Saintess, I did not lose. In a prolonged fight, the odds would have turned in my favor…”
The need to say so was itself evidence enough.
Dolores kept that thought to herself.
“Mozgus is one of the Empire’s relatively few high-level Graduators. Anyone who can overwhelm him so easily is difficult to understand.”
She studied Vikir’s mask.
Nothing useful could be read through it.
As Vikir looked down at the untouched tea, Dolores spoke again.
“I do not think you can drink that through the mask.”
Vikir silently agreed.
Since he had said very little, Dolores decided to move directly to the subject.
“I have been under a great deal of stress lately.”
Vikir looked up.
Was it academy work, family obligations, or something else?
Dolores shook her head.
“I am on vacation from both academy and family duties. The nobles and merchants you saw outside are the real problem.”
Wealthy visitors kept bringing illnesses to her and demanding personal treatment.
Most tried to purchase access with money, favors, or prestige.
“The conditions they ask me to cure are often self-inflicted or minor. There are people in genuine danger who need healing far more.”
If Dolores had limited holy power to spend, she would rather use it on people truly suffering.
“Justice, charity, equality, and divine will sound simple in sermons, but people become blind to everything beyond their immediate desires.”
Vikir nodded.
“Theology is ultimately the study of understanding human beings.”
He had heard Dolores say those exact words many times on future battlefields.
Vikir had only repeated an old memory, but Dolores’s eyes widened.
Mozgus reacted as well.
“Old Testament, Azmoth, chapter six, verse nine. That is not a common quotation.”
“….”
“You know old theological texts surprisingly well. Are you part of the Rune Church?”
Vikir paused.
He had little interest in Imperial theology, so changing the subject seemed preferable.
“More importantly, there is a plague developing in the slums of St. Mecca.”
“…Is your information reliable?”
“You examined the water I brought. It came from a slum well.”
“You had nothing to do with contaminating it?”
“Why would I voluntarily come here and warn you?”
Vikir continued.
“I do not want innocent people harmed. Especially not people who have no part in the conflict.”
“Oh? Are you from St. Mecca? I was born here too.”
Dolores’s eyes brightened briefly.
Vikir had not meant the statement literally and simply let the misunderstanding pass.
“I believe some things are absolute too. The outbreak needs to be stopped quickly.”
“A rough soul, but not an evil one.”
Dolores’s expression became serious.
“You told Mozgus that you saw suspicious people in the slums.”
“Yes. I saw people emptying vials into wells.”
“If the plague is deliberate, who would do it, and why?”
Dolores fell into thought.
An epidemic was never only a medical problem.
Institutions built around communal living, such as monasteries and schools, could be hit especially hard.
A major outbreak could reduce the number of trained clergy while simultaneously increasing demand for spiritual guidance.
Replacing them too quickly with inexperienced priests could create more heresies, cults, and superstition.
For Quovadis, an uncontrolled epidemic therefore carried major political as well as humanitarian risk.
That made early suppression essential.
Vikir answered briefly.
“Anyone who profits from weakening Quovadis.”
“…That still leaves too many possibilities.”
Dolores frowned.
Even one of the Academy’s brightest minds could not predict events that had not happened yet.
Vikir could, because he remembered a previous future.
He drew a map from inside his clothing.
The Seven Great Families were marked around the Imperial heartland.
Vikir pointed to several locations.
“The houses with the clearest potential benefit are Leviathan and Bourgeois.”
As a poison-and-medicine house, Leviathan could collect patient samples, study the disease, and potentially turn what it learned into another weapon.
A powerful epidemic also created enormous opportunities for a family built around toxins and treatment.
The weakening of an old rival like Quovadis would benefit Leviathan politically as well.
Bourgeois could profit in a different way.
Plague killed people, but property and stored wealth remained.
Dead workers and bankrupt owners could leave factories, farms, shops, guild interests, currency, and warehouses vulnerable to acquisition.
A sufficiently wealthy commercial house could buy those assets cheaply and use the enlarged capital base to dominate the recovery.
New fashions for newly rich survivors, health goods for people frightened by disease, and other post-crisis markets could then grow rapidly.
‘That would also explain why Bourgeois merchants wanted Ballak materials like medicinal plants and monster parts.’
Vikir kept the thought to himself.
‘The people who released the Red Death were probably connected to Leviathan.’
Bourgeois could also be cooperating for financial gain.
Information sharing between the two houses would fit what Vikir remembered.
Quovadis and the frontier tribes would bear the human cost.
“For those reasons, I suspect Leviathan and Bourgeois may be involved. It is only a hypothesis.”
Vikir’s actual confidence came from events he had personally experienced before regression, but he presented only an evidence-based inference.
Then—
There was almost no reaction from across the table.
“…?”
Vikir tilted his head.
Through the mask, he looked toward Dolores and Mozgus.
Both were frozen in place with their mouths slightly open.
