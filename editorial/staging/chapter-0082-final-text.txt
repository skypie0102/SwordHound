Aiyen dreamed.
Her earliest memory in the dream was fire, claws, and the pain of being torn apart.
Cerberus, the three-headed hound of hell.
The beast had emerged from the oil fields and attacked anyone who entered its territory.
As Madam Eight-Legs expanded her range, Ballak’s hunters had been forced into conflict with Cerberus.
They eventually drove the monster away, but the victory came at a heavy price.
Aiyen was mortally wounded and left behind during the fighting.
She had thought she would die there, even though most of her life should still have been ahead of her.
Instead, Imperial slavers found her, gave her only enough treatment to keep her alive, and sold her.
She was thrown into a cold cage and expected only death or a worse fate.
At the time, escape had seemed impossible.
Her freedom to roam the jungle was gone, and her honor as a Ballak warrior had been reduced to merchandise.
What would happen to her now?
Would she become a noble’s possession, a gladiatorial slave, or simply another body consumed by Imperial cruelty?
Her wounds festered, her fever rose, and days without proper water left both body and mind failing.
The proud hunter lay broken in the cage, certain she was dying.
Then a miracle appeared.
A boy arrived like a beam of white light.
He tore open the darkness surrounding Aiyen’s future.
Even through fading consciousness, she noticed his appearance, his strength, and the fearless composure she had never seen in anyone her age.
Could someone so young truly be that strong?
Not even among Ballak’s finest young warriors had she seen anyone like him.
She watched the slave traders who had treated her like livestock fall before him.
That bloody night changed everything.
The boy opened her cage and gave her the chance to return to the home she thought she had lost forever.
Aiyen never forgot his face.
Afterward, she crossed the Imperial border again and again for one reason: to find Vikir.
Entering the Empire meant risking her life.
Sometimes she searched with others and sometimes alone.
A girl raised in a tiny jungle community had never imagined how many Imperials there were.
But the boy had been extraordinary, so she believed that one day she would recognize him among the crowds.
One by one, other Ballak hunters completed the partner-seeking journeys expected of them.
Some returned with Imperial partners they had chosen, while others formed pairings in their own way.
Aiyen alone continued searching.
She waited for the person she had decided must someday reappear.
While others her age settled into partnerships, Aiyen remained alone.
She barely looked at anyone else.
Until one day—
Aiyen found the boy again.
It was unmistakably the same person who had saved her.
But another woman was in his arms.
For an instant, jealousy burned in Aiyen’s chest.
She forced herself to calm down.
Whatever relationship Vikir had with someone else belonged to a past Aiyen could not change.
What mattered, she decided, was whatever future might still be built.
The problem was that Vikir never behaved according to her plans.
He had become even stronger since their first meeting.
He fought Ballak warriors brilliantly and proved yet again that he was exceptional.
The more Aiyen watched him, the more strongly she wanted him to remain with her.
That feeling intensified when he charged Madam Eight-Legs without hesitation.
Ballak respected courage above almost everything, and Vikir had more than earned that respect.
When he was caught by the monster, Aiyen moved despite her own terror.
She wanted to save him even if doing so meant drawing Madam Eight-Legs’ attention.
How could anyone willingly face something so monstrous?
Her legs shook and cold sweat covered her body, but she acted anyway.
Her lasso caught Vikir and pulled him free of the monster’s reach.
Aiyen carried the ruined, barely conscious boy back to Ballak.
The decision caused immediate backlash.
Some elders wanted nothing to do with an Imperial outsider.
Rumors that Vikir might belong to Baskerville—Ballak’s hereditary enemy—made execution seem reasonable to several of them.
Aiyen refused.
She argued that he had once saved her life and that letting him live could benefit the tribe.
Her judgment proved correct.
The more Vikir recovered, the more useful he became to Ballak.
He hunted, brought back major prey, confronted exploitative merchants, and eventually found ways to protect the tribe from the Red Death.
The years of living together passed with startling speed.
Hunts, meals, arguments, healing, training, and the Fountain of Valor blurred together into a single bright stretch of memory.
At some point, Aiyen’s attachment stopped being simple possessiveness.
She truly wanted Vikir to stay.
Then—
“Sweetheart, wake up.”
Someone shook Aiyen gently.
She forced her heavy eyelids open.
The woven ceiling of a Ballak shelter came into focus.
Aiyen was in bed, drenched in sweat.
Her face was flushed, her temperature high, her appetite gone, and dehydration had left her weak.
With difficulty, she turned toward the person beside her.
Chieftain Akwilla was watching her daughter with concern.
Akwilla wiped Aiyen’s face with a damp cloth.
“You kept calling Vikir’s name in your sleep.”
“It hurts so much, Mother. Is this the Red Death?”
Aiyen struggled for breath.
Akwilla examined her carefully.
The symptoms were obvious even without any elaborate examination.
Aiyen was feverish and flushed.
But the characteristic red spots of the plague were absent.
Akwilla considered that.
“This doesn’t look quite like the Red Death. Maybe it is something else.”
Aiyen frowned and pressed a hand to her chest.
“It keeps getting worse. My chest hurts, my body is hot, and it feels as if my heart is drying out.”
“Hold on, daughter. Vikir will be back. You were the one who let him go.”
“I’m fine without him. He isn’t coming back.”
Her words were defiant, but the pain behind them was obvious.
Aiyen turned toward Akwilla almost pleadingly.
“Mother, I’ve never felt this miserable before. I’m hurting so badly that I keep thinking foolish things and blaming you for giving birth to me.”
Akwilla gathered her daughter into her arms.
For once she was not the Night Fox or Ballak’s chieftain, only a worried mother.
“My dear.”
Akwilla spoke softly.
“A long time ago, your father and I planted a very small seed.”
It was the first time Akwilla had told Aiyen anything substantial about her father, and even through the fever Aiyen listened.
Akwilla continued.
“Your father planted it, and I watered it every day. After some months it became a beautiful, healthy flower.”
She held Aiyen’s face in both hands.
Aiyen stared back through watery eyes.
For a moment the space between mother and daughter felt unexpectedly warm.
Then Akwilla finished the story.
“We used the flower in one of your father’s extremely foolish experiments, got badly intoxicated, and that night eventually led to you.”
“….”
“And your grandfather executed your father the next day for using drugs to play dangerous tricks.”
Aiyen looked visibly defeated by the revelation.
“…I think I feel worse now, Mother.”
“Wait a little longer. If Vikir returns with a cure—”
“Stop feeding me hope! Why would any slave come back after his leash was removed?”
At that moment their conversation was cut short.
A commotion erupted outside.
“They’re here!”
“Vikir! Vikir is back!”
“He brought a cure!”
Akwilla’s sharp hearing caught the shouts racing through the village.
Her face brightened.
“See, daughter? I told you—”
She never finished.
BANG!
Aiyen shot upright and smashed through the wall in her rush to leave.
The girl who had barely been able to move moments before climbed down from the shelter and ran at full speed.
Akwilla stared after her with her mouth hanging open.
“…Was that really the Red Death?”
