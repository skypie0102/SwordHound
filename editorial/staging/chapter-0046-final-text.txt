“So this is what we’re supposed to do together?”
Vikir looked down with a frown.
Across from him, Camus rested her chin in her hands and smiled brightly.
Bread, potatoes, boiled meat, and vegetables steamed on the table between them.
“You need to eat to stay strong. Here, have more.”
Camus pushed food toward him while Vikir pressed a hand to his forehead.
They had already been staying at the fortress for more than ten days.
During that time, Camus seemed to have recovered somewhat from the grief over Rosie’s death.
Unfortunately, her way of recovering had become a headache for Vikir: she now came to his quarters every day insisting that they eat together.
Five or six times a day.
“…Why do we need six meals a day?”
“Eating small amounts often is healthy.”
“I usually eat one large meal a day. I may skip the next one.”
“No. Skipping meals lowers combat strength. That violates military discipline.”
“You say that, but you barely eat anything yourself.”
Camus looked away awkwardly at the accusation.
She put a little salad in her mouth and chewed.
“I get full just watching you eat.”
“….”
Vikir chewed his meat and frowned.
He could not understand why a fifteen-year-old girl from another house whom he had not seen in seven years was so fixated on him.
‘Is this about an arranged marriage?’
Before regression, Camus had discussed strategic marriages with several potential partners but had never actually married any of them.
It would be troublesome if she had now chosen Vikir as a political-marriage candidate.
‘I intend to leave House Baskerville.’
And not merely leave it; eventually he intended to destroy it.
After doing every filthy job the family demanded, what had he received in return?
He still remembered the disgust and injustice of being framed, dragged to the execution ground, and made to wait beneath the Guillotine; that debt had to be repaid.
Which meant getting entangled with a daughter of House Morgue was best avoided.
The last thing he needed was some scandal or romantic complication later.
The same rule applied not only to Camus, but to every woman in the world.
Unaware of his thoughts, Camus asked innocently,
“Is there something disgusting in the meat?”
“…?”
“You’re making a face like you just chewed something awful.”
Vikir blinked a few times, then calmly picked up his fork and resumed eating.
He never once looked up.
Meanwhile,
Vikir’s complete lack of reaction felt strangely refreshing to Camus.
Usually, anyone she invited to eat with her—family or outsider, man or woman, young or old—was delighted.
Men from outside House Morgue in particular tended to look excited before they had even sat down.
It was only natural.
Camus was young, gifted, beautiful, and expected to wield enormous power in the future.
Yet Vikir ignored every bit of the social logic Camus had learned in fifteen years.
“I think we should eat separately from now on.”
“What? Why?”
“It’s uncomfortable.”
Vikir said it plainly and set down his fork.
Camus nodded as though she understood the complaint.
The dining hall where Vikir ate was indeed almost entirely filled with women.
The warriors taking meals and many of those serving them were women; hardly any men were visible.
Camus chuckled.
“House Morgue is matriarchal. Most of our combat personnel are women, while many of the men handle cooking and rear-line logistics.”
It was almost the opposite of the intensely male-dominated Baskervilles.
Just as male bodies held a slight advantage in swordsmanship, women tended to have a slight advantage in magic, so Morgue’s fighting force naturally skewed female.
The men instead handled much of the supply work in the rear, a military role no less important than fighting.
“My uncle, who oversees the supply side, is still suffering from that chest wound, so our provisions are tight. Eat while there’s food.”
Camus ignored Vikir’s suggestion that they dine separately.
Instead, she piled several boiled potatoes onto his plate and smoothly changed the subject.
“You don’t have these back home, do you?”
She was talking about the potatoes.
They were a new high-yield variety improved through Morgue’s plant magic: much more nutritious than ordinary potatoes and easier for the body to absorb.
The drawback was that the breeding had emphasized nutrition so heavily that the flavor suffered.
Camus chewed one of the dry potatoes.
“Nutritionally they’re a success. The only problem is the taste. Still better than the haggis you eat at home, right?”
Vikir stared at the potato for a moment, then picked up his fork.
It did not look as though he would be escaping Camus anytime soon.
This was Morgue territory, and she was the officer in charge.
For now, Vikir decided to follow her change of subject.
“The potato itself isn’t bad.”
“…Hm?”
Camus looked at him again.
Vikir lifted one of Morgue’s potatoes with a serious expression.
“I said the ingredient isn’t bad. You’re just cooking it badly.”
“What? The fortress cook used to work in the Imperial Capital. I brought him here because I’m picky about food. If even he can’t make these taste good, the problem is the potato.”
Vikir shook his head.
‘With the supply problem, we will be eating these for a while anyway.’
He took the potatoes and walked toward the kitchen.
Using a knife, Vikir cut them into pieces and lightly seasoned them with salt and vinegar.
Then he added dried mentrake leaves and sabik fruit juice before steaming the potatoes.
The fortress cooks saw what he was doing and hurried over in alarm.
“Hey, what are you doing—”
But Camus entered the kitchen and stopped the cook.
“….”
She watched Vikir’s hands closely.
A little later, Vikir held out a lightly steamed piece of potato.
“This removes the earthy smell and brings out the sweetness. It also restores something close to an ordinary potato’s texture.”
The cooks reacted all at once.
“You can prepare this variety like that?”
“That’s incredible. This breed is new—how did you know?”
“You’re right. The earthy taste is gone, and the texture is much better.”
The kitchen immediately adopted Vikir’s simple method.
Morgue’s warriors were delighted that their ration potatoes suddenly tasted better.
So were Staffordshire and the Baskerville triplets—Highbro, Middlebro, and Lowbro—who were eating among Morgue’s troops.
“Master, how did you know how to prepare those potatoes? Even House Morgue didn’t know.”
Staffordshire stared at him in surprise.
Vikir merely looked away instead of answering.
This variety of Morgue potato had only just been introduced.
Roughly a decade later, it would become a battlefield staple.
During the Age of Destruction, when supplies were desperately scarce, warriors fighting demons developed countless ways to make limited field rations more tolerable.
Veteran mercenaries learned how to draw every bit of flavor they could from simple ingredients, including methods for removing the astringency and earthy smell from potatoes.
Vikir had survived countless campaigns and naturally knew many such field recipes.
Some lessons had been learned through poisoning, hunger, and injury; others had produced unexpectedly good food.
Meanwhile,
“…!”
Camus’s eyes widened after tasting the newly prepared potatoes.
“Hey. Want to come over to Morgue and be our cook?”
“….”
“Actually, forget asking. I’ll just take you.”
Vikir let out a quiet sigh.
Camus’s eyes were still shining.
“Speaking of which, when are you entering the Academy? I’ll match your timing. I can probably arrange early admission by a year or two. Wouldn’t it be fun if we entered as first-years together…?”
Just then,
“…!”
The playfulness vanished from Camus’s eyes.
She stopped talking.
At the same moment,
“….”
Vikir fell silent too.
The atmosphere beyond the window had changed in an instant.
Both of them sensed the same ominous presence before anyone else in the fortress noticed.
Then,
Clang, clang, clang!
A piercing alarm bell rang outside.
It was the signal for a barbarian attack.
