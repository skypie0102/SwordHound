Vikir reached back into memories from his previous life.
The strange Imperial outsiders, their serpent-marked dagger, and the red liquid released into Ballak’s water all connected in his mind.
‘…Come to think of it, this happened around this time.’
The event was the Red Death.
A terrifying epidemic covered victims in red spots, caused vomiting and diarrhea, and slowly killed them.
It spread rapidly through the jungle and killed enormous numbers of frontier people.
Its scale eventually rivaled the great plagues once remembered inside the Empire.
The infection spread far enough to threaten Imperial territory as well.
In Vikir’s previous life, Camus Morgue eventually used large-scale fire magic to establish a barrier that helped halt its advance toward the Empire.
House Quovadis also sent Dolores, whose powerful holy abilities treated large numbers of sick people on the Imperial side.
Those measures protected the Empire far better than the tribes; the people of the depths still suffered catastrophic losses approaching forty percent.
The disaster had ultimately benefited Baskerville.
With major tribal predators removed from the mountain ecosystem, lower-ranking demons multiplied rapidly and monster waves caused severe civilian losses.
That instability increased Baskerville’s frontier importance and strengthened Hugo’s political position.
“I cannot let that happen again.”
Vikir intended to stop the epidemic before history repeated itself.
He had learned enough about Ballak during his years among them to know what was at stake.
Meanwhile—
Inside Ballak, the generations were still divided.
Older elders wanted ritual intervention, while younger people wanted to abandon the settlement before infection arrived.
“….”
Chieftain Akwilla remained silent with a deep frown.
She did not want to abandon a village containing generations of ancestral graves.
Shaman Ahmen understood exactly how strongly that sentiment worked in his favor.
“How can we abandon the sacred ground where our ancestors rest? We have lived here for nearly two hundred years!”
More than a hundred ancestral remains lay in sacred sites around the settlement.
For Ahmen and the elders aligned with him, abandoning those graves was one of the strongest arguments against migration.
Aiyen, representing the younger generation, refused to yield.
“And what happens if the disease comes and kills the children? What future is left if we protect the dead but lose the next generation?”
Child mortality was already a brutal fact of Ballak life.
Ballak families traditionally had many children over the course of adulthood.
Even then, a large portion of infants failed to survive early childhood.
Malnutrition, disease, warfare, and hunting accidents took many lives.
Pregnancy and childbirth also carried serious risks for mothers, keeping the tribe’s population fragile.
Adding the Red Death to those pressures could leave Ballak with no future at all.
Unable to watch the argument continue, Akwilla finally spoke.
“The first question is how the Red Death spreads.”
What exactly was the disease, and why did it cling so strongly to living creatures?
Without answering that, Ballak could not choose an effective response.
Akwilla’s question immediately produced many answers.
“A curse! It has to be a curse!”
“No, it spreads through the gaze!”
“The spirits of hunted animals are taking revenge!”
“Someone must have eaten the wrong mushroom!”
“The ancestors are angry because we neglected their graves!”
“There must be a poisonous insect!”
“The Forest God has abandoned us!”
“The Imperials brought the sickness here!”
Ballak people rarely liked answering a trusted question with “I do not know.”
They often preferred offering a confident guess.
The instinct came from goodwill and a desire not to disappoint the person asking, but in this crisis it produced more noise than help.
“…Hmm.”
Akwilla’s frown deepened.
Then a hand rose quietly.
It was Vikir’s. He met Akwilla’s eyes.
“I know a way to stop the Red Death.”
◆◆◆
About ten days later—
Clatter, clatter, clatter.
A demon tied with rope struggled violently.
It was a goblin, among the smallest and weakest humanoid demons.
The creature hung upside down with its limbs restrained as part of a controlled test.
[Grrrrrrrrrrrr!]
Ballak warriors repeatedly dipped the goblin into a large container of contaminated water.
After fighting wildly for a time, the goblin suddenly became subdued.
Red spots soon appeared across its skin.
It drooled, vomited, and stopped baring its teeth.
Then it began shuddering weakly as death approached.
Ballak’s warriors moved the infected goblin onto an oil-soaked pyre and burned it.
No one wasted sympathy on a creature known for abducting and eating human children.
The infected goblin soon died in the fire.
The hunters prepared another test subject.
Aiyen stood nearby holding the ropes.
“Vikir. Is the Red Death really carried by water?”
Vikir nodded.
“Yes. Contaminated water is the main route. Boil it first and the danger drops dramatically.”
“Really?”
Aiyen trusted him enough to test the claim immediately.
Another goblin was lowered toward a second container filled with water from the same source.
Then—
[Cackle!]
The goblin died immediately from the heat.
Vikir put a hand to his forehead.
“Let the water cool first.”
“Right.”
Aiyen slapped her own forehead in embarrassment.
They boiled another batch properly.
Only after the water cooled completely did they immerse the next goblin.
Ten days passed—the observed incubation period—and the goblin did not develop the Red Death.
Akwilla and the gathered Ballak warriors reacted with amazement.
“We found a way to resist the Red Death!”
“The answer was water.”
“So simply boiling drinking water can prevent it?”
“Vikir, you saved the tribe again!”
Praise and admiration poured over him.
Older and younger Ballak alike looked at Vikir with relief.
Vikir disliked becoming the center of attention.
Ballak custom, however, demanded enthusiastic praise, and the entire group crowded around him.
Vikir waved off the congratulations.
“Be careful with all contaminated water. Keep it out of the mouth and eyes, and be cautious around mist and spray from infected wetlands.”
Drinking water should always be boiled, and wetlands should be avoided whenever possible.
Those simple precautions sharply reduced infection risk.
Avoiding direct contact with waste and corpses from the sick was equally obvious.
“What do you not know?”
Vikir ignored Aiyen’s admiration.
Akwilla addressed the tribe.
“Send hunting birds to the neighboring tribes with Vikir’s warning: beware the water.”
Everyone agreed that the information should spread as widely as possible.
Akwilla rose and approached Vikir.
Vikir bowed silently.
When he first met her, her presence had felt like an entire mountain range pressing down on him.
Now that pressure was gone.
What remained felt warmer—closer to the welcome of a home and family.
Akwilla smiled gently.
“Because of you, I can finally see a path through this crisis. You have my deepest thanks.”
Nothing in her expression resembled the feared Night Fox.
Vikir answered only with another bow.
Then—
“But the rainy season is almost here. How can anyone avoid water then?”
Someone challenged the plan.
Shaman Ahmen stared at Vikir with a rigid expression.
He had already lost authority after mistaking the merchants’ narcotic poisoning for a curse.
Now Vikir had again contradicted him by proving that the Red Death was preventable disease rather than divine punishment, and Ahmen clearly felt his remaining position threatened.
This time, however, his practical objection was valid.
When the rainy season came, rivers would overflow and rain would fall constantly.
Moisture would saturate the air while aquatic animals and contaminated water spread across the jungle.
Boiling water for drinking and washing could not solve every exposure route.
The assembled warriors began murmuring.
Ahmen smiled slightly as uncertainty spread.
But—
“The flood-control work must be finished before the rainy season arrives.”
Vikir already had the next step prepared.
Large-scale drainage and flood control.
It was also necessary for his longer-term plans.
