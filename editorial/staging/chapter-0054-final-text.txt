Aiyen was the youngest hunting captain in Ballak.
She led the tribe’s younger hunters through the jungle without fear.
Yet even Aiyen had encountered an opponent who baffled her.
His name was Vikir, the slave she had recently taken during the raid on Imperial territory.
“….”
Aiyen stared blankly at Vikir.
Despite his battered body, Vikir moved quickly and built his own shelter as though he needed no help.
Scrape, scrape, scrape…
More than twenty wooden poles, each a little over two meters long, were arranged into a frame and sealed with a mixture of clay, stone dust, lime, and water.
The structure combined dry and wet building methods, with leaf-lined walls covered in animal hides.
A door and window were fitted quickly, and the floor was layered with wood chips and fallen leaves.
The small amount of building material issued to a slave proved more than enough.
In less than half an hour, Vikir had made a shelter fit for one person.
“Task complete.”
Vikir turned toward Aiyen, his voice flat and dry.
Aiyen’s first order had simply been: build your own home.
She peered inside the finished tent in disbelief.
A small opening in the ceiling could be closed when rain came.
A compact fire pit sat inside, with a smoke channel running beneath the floor and venting outside.
When a fire was lit for cooking, warm smoke traveled beneath the floor and heated the shelter from below.
Smoke escaped while residual heat remained, and the arrangement also provided good ventilation and light.
Aiyen was speechless.
“…How are you so good at building tents?”
She had actually expected to watch the injured Vikir struggle.
Aiyen quietly hid the extra materials she had prepared to offer once he ran into trouble.
There were sturdy poles, brick-making supplies, and oil for firing them.
Vikir had needed none of it; he found calcareous soil, carried it over, mixed it with water, and hardened it himself.
He also layered broad leaves into a barrier against wind and rain.
‘…It has been a while.’
Swordsmen who survived the Age of Destruction could not rely on swordsmanship alone.
They had to know enough survival craft to camp through crushing heat, bitter cold, and every other hostile environment.
Vikir therefore had considerable experience with ordinary domestic work as well.
After finishing the shelter, he sorted the hides piled near Aiyen’s tent and repaired torn cloth.
He cleared the nearby drainage ditch and split firewood for the night.
Chop, chop, chop…
He noticed chores and completed them before his supposed master even asked.
He was, in practical terms, an exceptionally capable slave.
“The foundation under the chieftain’s tent looks unstable.”
“…My mother’s tent?”
“Yes. When the rainy season comes, the soil beneath the center pole may shift and water will leak in. If you order it, I can mix the lime-rich soil here with stone powder from the opposite hill, form bricks, fire them with oil, and reinforce the base.”
“…Do that. I was thinking the same thing.”
Aiyen scratched her head.
Vikir did exactly as he had proposed.
Dragging his still-injured legs, he collected pale soil from one hill and stone from another, crushed the rock, and mixed the materials.
He added water into a workable clay, shaped rectangular bricks, baked them over fire, then treated and dried them with hyena oil.
The bricks reinforced the base of the chieftain’s tent and made the whole structure more stable.
Even Akwilla, initially annoyed by the disturbance, was satisfied with the result.
“So that was why the ceiling kept leaking no matter how many leaves and hides I added. The problem was the foundation.”
Akwilla still showed little personal interest in Vikir.
She simply ordered him to inspect, maintain, and repair other worn parts of the chieftain’s quarters.
Aiyen disliked having her slave spend so much time maintaining her mother’s tent, but did not object.
She hovered behind Vikir while he dug drainage channels around Akwilla’s shelter and fitted simple screens to keep leaves and mud from clogging them.
“Hey. Are you really fine doing all of that alone?”
Vikir would give some short reply and keep working.
“Then should the whole village come do it together?”
Whatever he said, Aiyen found another reason to keep talking.
“Do you actually enjoy working? You’ve been at it all day.”
“It isn’t even the rainy season and you’re already digging drains.”
“Here. You don’t have this back home, do you? Eat it.”
“…Your master is talking to you. Why don’t you answer?”
Vikir steadily ignored her whenever she distracted him, but Aiyen kept disappearing and reappearing around his work.
At this point, it was difficult to tell who was supposed to be master and who was slave.
Thump! Thump! Thump!
Vikir finished washing clothes with alkaline ash water and a wooden bat.
From expendable recruit to veteran, he had served as scout, herbalist, healer, cook, assassin, cleaner, launderer, and nearly everything else survival could demand.
‘I keep blaming everything on the Age of Destruction, but it really was that severe.’
Vikir briefly remembered those years while working.
“…Ahem. Hmm.”
Beside him, Aiyen quietly hid a torn leather skirt behind her back after her attempted demonstration proved less useful than Vikir’s method.
◆◆◆
Meanwhile,
the habits Vikir had built in the Age of Destruction made independent work almost effortless.
In fact, this quiet slave routine felt easier than life in Baskerville or Underdog, though one thing still puzzled him.
“Ah, that’s better.”
Aiyen, princess of Ballak,
had chosen the drainage channel Vikir dug as a convenient place to relieve herself.
“I was holding that for too long.”
“….”
“Master, is it really proper to stare at someone handling private business?”
Aiyen had settled right beside Vikir’s work area with no concern for his discomfort.
Vikir stared in disbelief.
“What are you doing?”
“Marking my territory. This is my slave’s house, so it is part of my space too.”
“Could you at least use some privacy?”
“Why be embarrassed about an ordinary bodily function? Our customs are different.”
Aiyen made no particular effort to hide what she was doing.
Vikir covered his face and turned away.
He suddenly remembered an old study of the tribes beyond Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain.
It had noted that some tribes followed very different standards of bodily privacy, tied to health, marriage, and communal custom.
‘…That was what it said.’
The custom was real and had its own cultural background, so Vikir tried not to judge it by Imperial standards.
“Even so, don’t do this beside my house.”
“Why? A few centuries ago, people in your Empire dumped waste from windows, walked filthy streets, and covered the smell with perfume.”
That was true enough.
Vikir had no useful response, so he stayed quiet.
By the time their exchange ended, the day’s chores were finished.
Ballak life was relaxed whenever there was no war or hunt, and industrious Vikir found slavery here oddly easier than combining nonstop training with work in Baskerville or Underdog.
Aiyen, however, watched him with a strange expression.
“Hey.”
“…?”
“Don’t you want to become a proper member of the tribe as soon as possible?”
What was she talking about?
Vikir frowned as Aiyen came closer and sat down.
Because her sense of personal privacy differed from his, Vikir kept his gaze firmly on her face.
Aiyen continued.
“Life as my slave isn’t so bad, is it?”
“Not particularly.”
“You’ve certainly learned how to answer properly. A slave should know better than to complain.”
“…No. I mean it really isn’t that bad.”
“How could endless dirty chores not be bad?”
Aiyen waved dismissively, clearly hating the thought of cleaning and laundry herself.
“Come on. Come with me.”
“…?”
“I’ll help you become useful enough to fit into the tribe.”
As a slave, Vikir had little room to refuse.
Aiyen tapped the collar still tied around his neck.
“If you do well, I’ll remove this too.”
That offer was genuinely tempting.
The rope had been rubbing irritatingly against his neck for days.
“…What do I have to do?”
Vikir asked, and Aiyen’s eyes brightened.
“Hunt.”
“…Hunt?”
“Something big.”
Apparently she wanted him to join a hunt for large prey.
Ballak obtained most of its food through hunting.
Feeding nearly three hundred people evenly could require more than two hundred kilograms of meat in a day.
That assumed they could supplement the meat with mushrooms, berries, and edible roots.
Recently, poor hunting had forced some warriors to live on little more than mushrooms and berries for days.
“If we bring back a large amount of meat at a time like this, earning your freedom would not be impossible.”
Vikir gave Aiyen a strange look.
Hearing the word “freedom” from his captor was somewhat ironic.
Still, it was not a bad offer.
“All you need to do is assist me. You seem capable of moving around and carrying light loads despite your injuries.”
Aiyen said it with an eager gleam.
Vikir could not tell whether bringing down large prey was truly her only objective.
“All right. Let’s go.”
For now, he chose to accept Aiyen’s proposal.
As her captive, he had few better options anyway.
Vikir nodded, and Aiyen immediately continued.
“We leave at dawn.”
“I understand. But why were you grinning so much just now?”
“What? When was I grinning?”
Aiyen’s self-conscious smile vanished the instant Vikir pointed it out.
…Something about her attitude was suspicious.
