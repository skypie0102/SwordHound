That day, Vikir became a hero among the Ballak.
He had exposed the supposed plague weakening the tribe as a narcotic deliberately distributed by foreign merchants to make Ballak easier to exploit.
He had also improved their fire-arrow tactics into a coordinated oil-barrel attack during the merchants’ retaliation.
Those deeds came on top of the oxbear hunt that had already helped relieve the village’s food shortage.
With all that behind him, opposition became difficult.
“I will permit you to enter the Fountain of Valor.”
Chieftain Akwilla granted Vikir’s request without hesitation.
Her daughter had pushed hard for the reward from the beginning.
Now there was more than enough justification for Akwilla to approve it openly.
Even elders who had objected before now looked at Vikir with approval.
“….”
Only the shaman Ahmen continued stroking his beard with visible displeasure.
Ahmen had repeatedly misdiagnosed drugged warriors as victims of curses.
Vikir’s discoveries made Ballak healthier while simultaneously making the shaman’s old explanations look foolish.
Ahmen’s influence had already begun to shrink.
That left him in no position to openly challenge the decision about the Fountain of Valor.
Aiyen leaned toward Vikir with an enormous grin, as though she wanted to whisper something.
“….”
She said absolutely nothing.
Vikir frowned and turned toward her.
“Did you have something to say?”
“No.”
“Then why lean over here?”
“I felt like it.”
Aiyen kept grinning.
Then she finally spoke again.
“Since we are talking, there is something else.”
“What?”
“That old man over there feels wrong.”
“Why?”
“…A hunter’s instinct.”
Aiyen’s instincts had been sharpened by years of hunting to the point that she sometimes sensed danger before she could explain it.
Vikir happened to agree with her suspicion.
The difference was that he could support his suspicion of Ahmen with evidence rather than instinct alone.
Ahmen had called the merchants’ narcotic effects a plague and a curse that only shamanic rites could cure.
That belief had strengthened his authority and made the tribe more dependent on him.
Yet even Aheul, a member of his own family, had suffered from the same contaminated goods.
Vikir also remembered what the Bourgeois merchants had said during the confrontation.
“You do not understand! This is sacred trade!”
“The God of the Forest approves this trade!”
“Even Ballak’s shaman blesses us in the Forest God’s name…!”
The last statement had been cut short, but it was enough to make Vikir suspicious.
He translated the merchants’ words into Ballak’s language, and Aiyen’s expression hardened instantly.
“That old fraud. If he has been helping outsiders exploit us, I will drag the truth out of him myself.”
“Calm down. Suspicion is not proof.”
“So we just watch him?”
Aiyen bristled, but Vikir answered only after thinking.
He lowered his voice and explained a plan; Aiyen listened with narrowed eyes.
“…You want me to do that?”
“Yes. Give him time. If he is involved, he will expose himself.”
“Hmm. That might work.”
Aiyen considered the idea.
Long deliberation was not really in her nature.
“Fine. We lose nothing by trying it your way. For now, we focus on what matters.”
Vikir nodded.
Restoring his damaged body remained the immediate priority.
◆◆◆
Later that night—
A full moon rose over the jungle.
The wilderness seemed to have swallowed the previous night’s bloodshed without a trace.
The bright moon hung over the trees as the jungle settled into silence.
Sssssss…
A light wind passed through the leaves.
Then the ground shifted faintly, rock split apart, and hot water began bubbling up from below.
The Fountain of Valor.
Ballak tradition said the spring had been blessed by Adonai, the legendary archer of their distant past.
Only a small number of honored Ballak warriors were permitted to use it.
Vikir stood alone before the spring.
Steam rolled from mineral-rich water that smelled faintly of sulfur.
Small animals had already gathered in the shallows.
None fled when Vikir approached.
Inside the spring, predator and prey seemed to observe an unspoken truce.
Vikir prepared to enter the water.
He eased past the rabbits, hares, and hedgehogs gathered near the edge and settled into the spring.
“…!”
The effect was immediate.
The unstable condition inside his body began calming almost as soon as the heat reached him.
“So the old shaman was not exaggerating the spring’s value.”
His mana clarified while damaged muscles and bones gradually settled back into proper alignment.
The sensation reminded him faintly of Baskerville’s River Styx.
The Fountain of Valor was somewhat weaker than the Styx, but unlike the river, it had no age restriction.
Its great limitation was availability: the spring could only be used on rare full-moon nights, perhaps once or twice a year.
Vikir let himself soak while his thoughts wandered.
It had been some time since he disappeared from Imperial society.
He wondered how Baskerville and Underdog City were functioning without him.
They were probably managing well enough.
‘Chihuahua is probably making a fuss, though.’
Vikir smiled faintly and shifted his gaze.
Then something unexpected caught his attention.
Even Vikir was momentarily surprised.
“Surprised?”
Aiyen had entered the spring without him noticing.
She had approached so quietly that even his sharpened senses had missed her.
Vikir immediately focused on the useful part of what had happened.
‘I failed to detect her approach. That stealth technique is worth learning.’
It could be valuable later.
While Vikir analyzed the movement—
“….”
Aiyen sighed at his complete lack of reaction.
“You did not even notice me until I spoke, did you?”
“You move quietly all the time.”
“Not like that.”
Vikir failed to see why she considered the distinction important.
Rather than argue, he turned back toward the water.
Aiyen settled nearby, produced a flask, and spoke.
“Give thanks to Adonai. He discovered this spring.”
“Adonai. I know the name.”
“You should. He was the one archer said to have fought the Madam directly. You are Ballak now, so show some respect—and some pride in inheriting his spirit.”
Two parts of that statement caught Vikir’s attention.
First, Ballak treated descent as a matter of inherited spirit and belonging, not merely blood.
Second, Adonai had apparently been strong enough to confront Madam Eight-Legs.
“An archer capable of fighting that monster alone…”
Vikir could barely imagine how far above his current level such an archer must have stood.
Then—
Aiyen turned toward him.
She leaned a little closer.
Drink had already reddened her face.
“Those merchants.”
“Hmm?”
“I knew something was wrong with them.”
Her face reddened further as she remembered bargaining a diamond up from one sack of corn to two.
“I knew the trade was suspicious. I just did not understand how badly they were cheating us. Even without you, I would have lost my temper eventually.”
“I know.”
Vikir laughed quietly.
Aiyen frowned.
“Do not laugh. Everyone knows diamonds are worth more than corn in the Empire.”
“How much more?”
Vikir asked, and Aiyen thought hard.
Her answer came without much confidence.
“One diamond is worth… at least ten ears of corn?”
“….”
“Twenty?”
“….”
“…Thirty?”
Vikir laughed again.
When he finally told her how much a diamond could buy in Imperial markets, Aiyen stared at him in horror.
“That is insane. It is a stone. Corn keeps people alive.”
“People value how it looks.”
“Morning dew sparkles too.”
“Diamonds are also extremely hard and durable.”
“Hit one wrong and it chips. Burn anything hot enough and it changes. What exactly is eternal about it?”
For once, Vikir had no immediate answer.
What, exactly, separated the values of so-called civilization from those of people the Empire called barbarians?
He found himself considering a question he normally would have dismissed as trivial.
Meanwhile—
“….”
Aiyen had drifted into thoughts of her own.
She had come to the spring with another purpose besides drinking.
Too proud to ask directly about Vikir’s future in Ballak, she had hoped for a quiet opportunity to discuss it without the entire village listening.
His standing in the tribe had risen sharply, and Aiyen clearly wanted a say in what happened once he fully recovered.
Akwilla had even encouraged her to speak honestly with him.
That was why Aiyen had brought her favorite drink and joined Vikir at the spring.
But—
“Doesn’t another person reduce the spring’s healing effect? Why did you come in when the chieftain said the spring was reserved for me tonight?”
Vikir, naturally, focused on efficiency instead.
Aiyen stared at him in disbelief.
“…Does that really matter right now? A true Ballak does not obsess over such tiny details.”
“I am not arguing. I am asking. If you do not want to answer, you do not have to.”
Vikir turned away again, and Aiyen’s eyebrow twitched.
“…Then prove you are recovering as well as you claim.”
For a moment—
Splash!
Water burst upward.
Aiyen rose and snapped back at Vikir’s calm expression.
Vikir still looked composed, though the sudden move had surprised him.
Aiyen grinned at finally getting a reaction and splashed water toward him.
“Stay still, slave.”
“….”
“Your master is checking whether you are actually recovering.”
At that exact moment—
Splish, splash, splish!
A chorus of splashes erupted beyond the steam.
Small silhouettes came running through the mist.
“Captain!”
“Brother Vikir!”
A group of children rushed into the spring.
“????”
Aiyen stared at them in disbelief.
Vikir turned toward the commotion as well.
Ballak’s children were pouring into the hot spring.
Many of them were still recovering from the effects of the merchants’ narcotics.
They hurried straight toward Aiyen and Vikir.
“Captain, play with us!”
Aiyen finally burst out.
“You are reducing the spring’s effect too! Who told all of you to come here?”
“The chieftain!”
The children answered together. Aiyen slapped a palm to her forehead.
Her mother had anticipated her perfectly.
Even Akwilla had joined in.
“That sounds different from what you said earlier about true Ballak ignoring small details.”
Vikir pointed it out with complete seriousness.
Aiyen let out a long sigh.
The children were already playing with the rabbits, hedgehogs, and squirrels that had gathered around the spring.
Vikir simply returned to soaking and let the healing continue.
At last Aiyen gave up on having any private conversation and spread her hands in surrender.
“Fine. Welcome, officially, to Ballak.”
At that point, she had nothing else to say.
