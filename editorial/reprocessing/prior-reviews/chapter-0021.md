## Camus's accusation

Camus's blunt accusation—asking Hugo whether he is the thief who stole Morgue's ruby mine—freezes the room. Preserve the comedy of an eight-year-old repeating something she heard Adolf say roughly one hour and forty-two minutes earlier while also preserving the genuine political tension beneath it.

Camus is not inventing the dispute. Adolf tries to frame her words as childish repetition, but later admits that the underlying claim is not baseless.

## Ruby-mine geography and ownership problem

The mine lies in the long-disputed border / joint-security area between Baskerville and Morgue. The physical problem is:

- the accessible surface entrance lies on Morgue-controlled land;
- the valuable underground ruby vein extends beneath Baskerville-controlled territory;
- following the vein from Morgue's entrance inevitably means tunneling under Baskerville land.

This is why both families can make a plausible ownership claim. Morgue needs ruby for magical research; Baskerville cares far more about territorial sovereignty than the gemstone itself.

Use the project's established **Red Fang Mountain** ruby-dispute terminology where a mountain name is required. The Korean/MTL cycle among Red Mountain/Red Peak/Red Spire and other machine-corrupted variants is not evidence for multiple distinct mines.

The recovered info-window label `Imaginary view of Camus mine` should be treated as a diagram caption, not as a separate named location.

## Covert mining and bloodshed

Morgue repeatedly tries covert tunneling. Baskerville hounds detect the encroachment and raid the work sites, producing several unpublicized violent clashes. Outwardly the families maintain a cordial relationship while privately resenting one another.

Adolf's visit is therefore diplomatic theater: the annual exercise is real, but the ruby dispute and resulting bloodshed are also core reasons for the visit.

## Adolf using Camus as political cover

Hugo recognizes that Adolf benefits from having a child state the most provocative claim. If Hugo pressures Camus too aggressively, Adolf can accuse the Empire's Sword Star of bullying an eight-year-old. Preserve that political trap; do not reduce the scene to a simple children's argument.

Hugo's problem is asymmetrical: rubies matter much more to Morgue than to Baskerville, while allowing Morgue access matters greatly to Baskerville's territorial pride.
