The harvest festival was in full swing.

Ballak village had become one enormous feast.

The tribe was lively on ordinary days, but the abundance of meat made this celebration louder than usual.

When thick jungle vines were cut, clear water poured from them and filled a massive stone pot.

Into the boiling water went anything edible the hunters had brought back:

sea crabs, freshwater crabs, turtles, softshell turtles, carp, crucian carp, venison, rabbit, squirrel, snake, larvae, beetles, ants, frogs, lizards, water plants, berries, mushrooms, roots—

everything.

The huge cauldron boiled in the center of the village.

Small game went into the communal broth.

Every villager received a bowl.

Larger prey such as deer and boar were roasted separately around the central fire while people sang and danced around them.

But none of it drew as much attention as the enormous carcass beside the stone pot.

The Oxbear.

A one-ton monster whose usable meat alone weighed more than eight hundred kilograms after removing bones and hide.

It was the true centerpiece of the festival.

Naturally, the hunters responsible for bringing it down received the respect, gratitude, and admiration of the whole village.

Aiyen and Vikir.

“How does it feel to be a hero, slave?”

Vikir did not answer.

Aiyen grinned and slapped his back.

“Oxbears are honorable prey. Strong, vicious, and clever. Nothing like boars—dim eyes, simple movements, panicking so badly when they run that they lose control of their bowels.”

She said the last part loudly.

A groan came from beside the roasting fire.

Ahun.

He had returned with an impressively large boar.

“Damn it. How did someone half-dead manage to kill that thing? Ridiculous luck.”

He spat and glared at Vikir.

Then Ahun began butchering the Oxbear for distribution.

Blood and organs spoiled fastest, so they went immediately into boiling pots.

The meat was cut by part and portioned among the villagers.

Eventually Ahun handed a piece to Vikir.

Vikir looked down.

His portion was noticeably smaller than everyone else's.

Even frail elders and small children had received more.

“What are you doing?”

“……?”

Ahun stared back.

“What are *you* complaining about, slave?”

“Why is my share so small?”

“Because your honor is too high!”

Vikir looked genuinely puzzled.

Aiyen burst out laughing.

“It's Ballak tradition. The hunter who contributed the most receives the least meat. The missing share is replaced by honor. Trading meat for honor is considered a great bargain.”

Ahun nodded.

“Damn right. I'd rather give you more meat, but rules are rules.”

Judging from his tone, he actually meant it.

More honor meant less meat.

Vikir personally preferred food to ceremonial prestige, but there was no point arguing.

*It doesn't matter.*

He could feed himself easily enough.

Besides, the true reward from the hunt was not meat.

The Oxbear had become Beelzebub's newest stored prey.

The Hellhound soul was gone.

In its place sat the stronger Oxbear ability.

**Kilogram Hammer** was simple.

While Vikir held his breath, his body weight could temporarily increase to roughly six hundred kilograms.

An extremely practical close-combat ability.

The karma from killing the Oxbear had also poured into his body, accelerating his recovery.

A new skill.

More experience.

Vikir was mentally tallying the results when Aiyen interrupted him.

“Heh. Look at Ahun's face. Isn't it hilarious?”

She had brought Vikir his proper hunter's allotment:

several kilograms of foreleg meat, a bottle of blood, and the heart, liver, and gallbladder reserved for hunters credited with major contributions.

Aiyen lifted the meat in front of him.

“Hunting is only half the work. Processing and preserving the kill matters just as much.”

She clearly intended to use the lesson as an excuse to supervise everything he did.

What Aiyen did not know was that Vikir had processed countless monster carcasses during the Age of Destruction.

Skin.

Organs.

Meat.

Bone.

Hide.

He was nearly a professional.

SCRAPE. SCRAPE. SCRAPE.

Vikir soaked a broad hide in water and scraped away the remaining fat with a sharp stone blade.

The skin came off cleanly and was set aside for tanning.

Once dried, it hardened like wood.

Oil squeezed from the animal's fat glands and repeated beating softened it again.

Meat, organs, and blood went into pots.

Rendered fat was skimmed for lamps.

Bones, claws, and teeth became tools, weapons, or ornaments.

Different cuts received different treatment.

Raw.

Boiled.

Stewed.

Blanched.

Smoked.

Sun-dried.

Vikir was particularly good at smoking large pieces whole.

Before long, villagers who had come merely to watch were handing him their own portions to prepare.

Aiyen included.

He tied salted cords around large cuts and hung them from branches.

Then he dug a pit, suspended meat inside it, and built a fire from pine needles, oak, and apple wood.

Branches, leaves, and mud sealed the opening.

Heat hardened the muddy lid.

Later, Vikir cracked it open and pulled up a whole golden-brown Oxbear hind leg.

The aroma spread through the village.

Ballak people praised it immediately.

Aiyen cut off a piece, tasted it, and stared at him.

“What *can't* you do?”

For someone whose soul had lived through the Age of Destruction, none of this was remarkable.

For someone whose body was only fifteen, it looked absurdly accomplished.

Vikir watched children blow on hot strips of meat and chew with determined expressions.

Seeing children run around full of energy had always been reassuring.

Ballak also had an unusual belief about sharing specific body parts.

Those with poor eyesight ate the eyes.

Those with weak lungs ate lungs.

Those with leg problems ate leg meat.

Those with pancreatic trouble ate the pancreas.

They believed consuming the matching body part of prey could strengthen one's own.

*Strange superstition.*

“Hey, slave.”

Aiyen had disappeared during the butchering.

Now she stood behind him again.

Her cheeks were flushed.

She held a large coconut-shell bowl filled with steaming broth and floating fat.

Something long and thick rested beneath the surface.

*An organ?*

The oil made it difficult to identify.

Aiyen cleared her throat.

“The shaman wanted this. I claimed it by hunter's right. You have to eat it.”

“I already received meat, blood, and organs.”

“No. This one is important.”

Her logic followed Ballak superstition.

“If your eyes are bad, eat the eyes. If your heart is bad, eat the heart. If your arms or legs are bad, eat the forelegs or hind legs.”

Vikir looked into the bowl.

“What part is this?”

Aiyen coughed and refused to answer.

“You've been sleeping in freezing places. You obviously need this. Just eat it.”

The young women nearby immediately started heckling her.

“Captain! Claiming the good part for yourself already?”

“And feeding that to your slave—who's that supposed to help?”

“The long-night season is coming soon.”

“That's not subtle at all!”

“Shut up!”

Aiyen raised a fist.

“If you want one so badly, go out with a lasso and catch your own!”

The jeering died at once.

Vikir frowned.

He still did not understand what they were making such a fuss about.

A gift was a gift.

He drank the hot broth and, under Aiyen's insistence, chewed and swallowed the dense piece at the bottom.

*Tough.*

The texture reminded him somewhat of haggis from Baskerville, only chewier and more acidic.

GULP.

His throat moved.

Aiyen finally relaxed.

“Good. Now maybe you'll be useful for something.”

Vikir frowned again.

*I was already useful on the hunt.*

He had no idea what else she expected.
