# Chapter 67: Unfair Trade (4)

> Chinese-primary reconstruction from `source/chinese/chapters/067.txt` — `b596ba76138b23c6f401ee7b21b2a2a9ae9e1943`. Recovered English Chapter 66 is the verified shifted secondary witness. Established terminology controls **Akwilla**, **Aiyen**, **Ah'Heman**, **Aheul**, **Ahun**, **Divine Archer Adonai**, **Madam Eight-Legs**, **Fountain of Valor**, **River Styx**, **Underdog City**, and **Chihuahua Baskerville**. This chapter establishes Aheul as Ah'Heman's granddaughter, resolving the earlier son/grandson drift. Because Vikir and Aiyen are under eighteen in current source chronology, the hot-spring relationship material is kept strictly nonsexual and non-erotic while preserving plot intent and interruption.

Vikir became a Ballak hero.

He had exposed the mysterious illness weakening the tribe as the result of cheap narcotics distributed by foreign merchants.

He had stopped the predatory trade.

Then he had turned Ballak fire arrows into a barrel-oil attack that destroyed the mercenary camp.

All of that came on top of helping bring down an Oxbear during the tribe's food shortage.

Akwilla finally had more than enough justification.

“I grant you access to the Fountain of Valor.”

The chieftain approved Vikir's request.

Even elders who had opposed him before were now smiling.

Only Ah'Heman looked dissatisfied.

The shaman had previously described the drug-induced sickness as a curse.

Vikir's discovery weakened that explanation.

As the villagers recovered, Ah'Heman's authority diminished.

He could no longer object as forcefully to Vikir entering the sacred spring.

Aiyen walked over with a broad grin and leaned close to Vikir's ear.

She said nothing.

Vikir turned.

“Do you have something to say?”

“No.”

“Then why are you leaning into my ear?”

“I felt like it.”

Her grin remained.

Then she finally spoke.

“Since I'm here—there's something about Ah'Heman.”

“What?”

“I think he's a traitor.”

“Why?”

“Instinct.”

Aiyen's hunting instincts were sharp enough that she sometimes sensed danger before she could explain it.

Vikir had reached the same suspicion by a different route.

Ah'Heman had called the merchants' narcotic effects a plague or curse that required shamanic rituals.

The fear had strengthened his authority.

And yet his own granddaughter, **Aheul**, had suffered from the same drug-related illness.

There was another detail.

Vikir remembered what the merchants had shouted when the market collapsed.

*This is sacred trade.*

*The Forest God protects it.*

And the sentence one merchant had stopped halfway through:

*Ballak's shaman blesses us on behalf of the Forest God…*

Vikir translated those words for Aiyen.

Her expression hardened.

“So he really may be tied to outsiders.”

“Suspicion is not proof.”

“Then we just watch him?”

Vikir lowered his voice and explained a plan.

Aiyen listened.

“……You want me to do that?”

“Yes. Wait. If there is a connection, they'll expose it themselves.”

Aiyen considered the idea.

Not for long.

“Fine. We lose nothing by waiting. For now, focus on what matters.”

Vikir agreed.

His recovery came first.

◆◆◆

Night fell under a full moon.

The jungle had already swallowed the previous night's violence.

A breeze moved through the leaves.

Then stone shifted.

Cracks opened.

Hot water rose from below.

The **Fountain of Valor**.

A sacred Ballak spring associated with **Divine Archer Adonai**.

Few warriors received permission to enter.

Vikir stood at the edge.

Steam carried the smell of minerals.

Small animals had already settled into the shallows.

Rabbit.

Mole.

Hedgehog.

Predators and prey ignored one another here as though the spring had its own truce.

Vikir entered the water.

The effect was immediate.

Mana settled.

Bones aligned.

Damaged muscle relaxed and began recovering faster.

The effect reminded him of the River Styx.

Somewhat weaker—

but without the Styx's age restriction.

The limitation was availability.

The spring emerged only once or twice a year on nights of the full moon.

Vikir rested in the water and thought about the places he had left behind.

Baskerville.

Underdog City.

*Chihuahua is probably making a fuss.*

He almost smiled.

Then Aiyen appeared beside the spring so quietly that Vikir had not sensed her approach.

That caught his attention more than anything else.

*Even my senses missed her.*

A technique worth learning.

Aiyen looked disappointed that his reaction was purely tactical.

“That's all you're thinking about?”

“What else?”

She sighed and settled into the spring at a respectful distance.

“Thank Adonai. He's the one who found this place.”

“Adonai. I know the name.”

“Of course. He was the only warrior said to have fought the Madam directly. You're Ballak now too. Respect him.”

Vikir considered that.

Ballak's concept of descent was not always literal blood.

Spiritual inheritance mattered just as much.

The second point was more striking.

Adonai had been able to confront Madam Eight-Legs directly.

*How strong was he?*

Vikir was nowhere near that level yet.

Aiyen changed the subject.

“Those merchants.”

“Yes?”

“I knew they were cheating us.”

Her face reddened as she remembered demanding two ears of corn for a diamond.

“I had the general idea. You just gave me the chance to hit back.”

Vikir smiled.

“Don't laugh. I knew diamonds were worth more than corn.”

“How much more?”

Aiyen thought.

“One diamond is worth ten ears of corn.”

Vikir said nothing.

“Twenty?”

Silence.

“……Thirty?”

Vikir laughed.

When he explained the true Imperial value, Aiyen looked genuinely horrified.

“That's insane. It's a rock. Corn is food.”

“It shines.”

“So does morning dew.”

“It's hard and lasts a long time.”

“Scratch it, hit it, burn it—everything breaks eventually. What's eternal about it?”

For once, Vikir had no answer.

What exactly separated civilization from barbarism?

The question occupied him longer than he expected.

Aiyen, meanwhile, had come to the spring for another reason.

The village was already treating Vikir as a potential permanent member and mate.

Aiyen had decided she would rather make her own feelings and intentions clear than let the village decide for her.

Akwilla had encouraged her to speak honestly.

But Vikir remained focused on recovery.

“If you're using the spring too, doesn't that reduce the effect? The chieftain only gave me permission.”

Aiyen stared at him.

“Is that really what you're worried about?”

“I'm only asking.”

She looked ready to argue—

when a burst of splashing came from the far side of the spring.

Children poured through the steam.

“Vikir!”

“Aiyen!”

They were the children who had suffered most from the merchants' drugs.

Akwilla had apparently sent them here to recover as well.

Aiyen slapped a hand to her forehead.

“You're reducing the spring's effect! Who told you to come?”

“The chieftain!”

Aiyen groaned.

Her mother had outmaneuvered her.

Vikir looked at her.

“You just said a true Ballak doesn't complain about small things.”

Aiyen sighed.

The children were already playing among the rabbits, hedgehogs, and squirrels in the warm shallows.

Vikir returned his attention to recovery.

Aiyen gave up on whatever conversation she had planned.

“Fine.”

She opened her arms toward the noisy spring.

“Welcome to Ballak.”

It was the only ending she could think of.
