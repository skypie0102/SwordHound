# Chapter 8: Hounds of Hell (1)

Until the age of fifteen, the children of the Baskerville Clan underwent two evaluations every year: a midterm and a final examination.

Their ordinary education was already brutally strict, partly because the clan had long been infamous as a family of berserkers.

But the real reason for that reputation was the practical examination.

It was so severe that calling it cruel hardly felt like an exaggeration.

Even children who had only just turned eight or nine were not exempt.

Baskerville children received merciless early training.

From the moment they could walk on two feet, they were made to run endlessly across broad training grounds and steep mountain trails to build basic strength.

Outside designated rest periods, they were not even allowed to lie down or sprawl on the ground.

Guardian knights serving as instructors drove them without mercy.

During breaks, the children played with rubber knives.

When they slept, they were sometimes placed beside the corpses of enormous monsters or the young of smaller monsters.

The purpose was simple: accustom them to the grotesque appearance of prey and the savagery of wild beasts.

At eight years old, formal combat training began.

Their diet changed as well.

Chicken bones were boiled into broth, then mixed with assorted scraps of meat, sinew, and offal and cooked down together.

The mixture was stuffed into tough intestines and boiled again until even the last of the excess fat had been drawn out.

Eaten at that stage, it became the ordinary high-calorie food known as haggis.

For preservation, it could be salted and dried until hard, becoming the jerky Baskerville children carried as field rations.

Today, every eight- and nine-year-old Baskerville child had been given a pouch of that dried ration and sent into the mountains beyond the clan’s settled grounds.

Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain.

It was a harsh land where only particularly vicious monsters could survive.

Climate, terrain, and ecosystem alike were barren and unforgiving.

The young hounds of Baskerville were thrown into this place and ordered to survive for roughly one month.

Naturally, the children were permitted to operate only inside a limited zone.

At the borders of that zone stood Baskerville guardian knights known as Guide Dogs.

The children abandoned in that unfamiliar environment were given two tasks.

‘Survive.’

‘And, if possible, hunt monsters that are large and strong.’

Originally the assignment had been nothing more than survival.

The current patriarch, Hugo Le Baskerville, had judged that too easy and added the second condition.

Every so often, a Baskerville child would find a safe cave or burrow and simply live on dried rations until the month ended.

‘A child who bravely fights monsters and survives cannot receive the same evaluation as a coward who hides in a hole and does nothing but eat.’

That was Hugo’s view.

There was no score of one hundred.

A swordsman died the moment he became complacent.

That was Hugo’s principle.

… Of course, death meant zero points under any circumstances.

Among all forms of death, a meaningless death was the most worthless of all.

Eight-year-old Vikir had arrived at Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain that day.

The moment he stepped down from the carriage, reddish-brown mounds of earth stretched before him.

Black ants crawled across the cracked ground.

A dry wind swept through dead old trees.

Everywhere he looked, there was only red and black.

The guardian knights addressed the children.

“Each young master has been given a badge. Once you leave this starting point, you may steal one another’s badges. Taking another child’s badge earns additional points.”

“Killing monsters also earns bonus points. Serious injury costs points. Death costs all of them—though by then your score will no longer be your greatest concern.”

“We strongly advise you not to leave the restricted zone. You are to move only through areas we have cleared over the last several days. Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain still contains many unknown forbidden regions.”

The guardian knights continued explaining smaller rules.

To Vikir, it was little more than irritating noise.

‘It’s been a long time.’

He had visited these mountains many times before.

As a child taking the examination.

Later, as an instructor.

And later still, as a hound carrying out the family’s work.

When he had first come here as a student, the place had seemed strange and terrifying.

When he returned as an instructor, however—

‘It was even more frightening and unfamiliar.’

That was because instructors had to search beyond the safe area.

The zone in which Baskerville children were allowed to survive was practically a comfortable cradle.

The truly dangerous territory lay beyond the boundary guarded by the Guide Dogs.

The forbidden zone.

Past the ridge that formed the edge of the children’s ‘cradle’, beyond several more mountain ranges, lived monsters and barbarian tribes powerful enough to make ordinary people despair.

Ever since the Baskervilles had claimed this land, they had steadily pushed their territory farther into Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain.

They did so with the emperor’s orders and support.

Every expansion of Baskerville territory also expanded the Rok Empire itself, and the clan was rewarded with tax privileges and broad autonomy over military facilities.

Before his regression, Vikir had spent years here as an examinee, instructor, and hunting dog.

He knew this environment extremely well.

Especially the ‘cradle’ on the inner side of the ridge.

Most of the children around him were not nearly so comfortable.

Dry cliffs crumbled underfoot.

A pungent wind blew through burned forests.

Ash drifted through the air.

From somewhere beyond sight came ominous gazes and cries.

“Ugh… This is my first time somewhere this unsettling.”

“It’s even more barren than last year.”

“You expect us to stay here for a whole month!?”

The eight-year-olds looked miserable.

The nine-year-olds pretended to be calm, but their expressions were no better. Their memories of the previous year were clearly unpleasant.

In practice, this examination was also a struggle between eight-year-olds trying to protect their badges and nine-year-olds trying to take them.

For the current eight-year-olds, who would become the hunters next year, this experience was vital.

In the middle of all that tension, Vikir stood quietly and waited for the bell announcing the start.

Several nine-year-olds nearby stared hungrily at his badge and grinned.

“That’s him, right? The kid who supposedly stayed under the River Styx for seven minutes.”

“Seriously? I heard he strangled two venomous snakes in his cradle too.”

“He’s the one who got a perfect score on the written exam? Let’s see how he does when things get physical.”

The whispers spread around him.

Yet there was one strange thing.

“…”

“…”

“…”

Highbro, Middlebro, and Lowbro—the three strongest and fiercest boys in the nine-year-old class—were completely silent.

They had always bragged that they would teach Vikir a lesson.

The other nine-year-olds could only look at them in confusion.

Before long, several children stepped forward to provoke Vikir themselves.

“You arrogant little bastard. The moment the practical starts, I’ll smash your head in with a rock.”

“You’re nothing special, so stop acting like you are.”

“The next month is going to feel like hell for you.”

At last, Vikir responded.

“I don’t think I’m impressive.”

His unexpected humility made the other children blink.

Then Vikir continued.

“… You’re all just too weak.”

A storm of crude curses immediately flew back at him.

“You—just wait!”

“Come to the old tree tonight, bastard!”

“Stay out of this! He’s mine! Don’t blame me if he dies!”

Even the youngest Baskerville pups answered petty provocation by baring half-grown fangs.

To an old hound who had survived countless life-or-death battles, it was not even amusing.

Then—

Ding—

Pavlov Van Baskerville, the guardian knight and instructor serving as the examination’s Guide Dog, rang the bell.

The practical examination began.
