# Chapter 65: Unfair Trade (2)

> Chinese-primary reconstruction from `source/chinese/chapters/065.txt` — `aa4cf365529795b9642b925c64ceebb393f978c9`. Recovered English Chapter 64 is the verified shifted secondary witness. Established terminology controls **Bourgeois Clan**, **Ballak Tribe**, **Underdog City**, **Cindywendy**, **Aiyen**, **Ahun**, **Aheul**, **Sir Smuggler**, and **Vikir's Special Law**. The merchants' illicit drug distribution and racial/sexual abuse are preserved as wrongdoing without gratuitous reproduction.

“This trade is void.”

The merchants froze.

The soot-covered stranger they had dismissed as a Ballak native had just spoken flawless Imperial.

“You speak Imperial?”

“Did you hear what we said?”

“There may have been a misunderstanding.”

“Let us explain—”

They had been insulting Ballak skin color and sexually harassing a village girl only moments earlier.

Vikir answered without expression.

“You mean the racial insults and harassment?”

The merchants went pale.

“If Ballak's warriors understood—”

“That's not the important issue now.”

The word Vikir had used was **void**.

The entire transaction was canceled.

The merchants' stock was still gathered in one place.

Ballak's trade goods were piled nearby.

Everything could simply be returned.

Aiyen sensed that Vikir had a reason and immediately stopped the market.

The merchants erupted.

“What are you doing?”

“You'll lose out if you cancel!”

“We don't come here every day!”

Vikir looked at them.

“Who loses more if the deal fails?”

Aiyen lowered her voice.

“People here have been waiting for these merchants.”

She was right.

Villagers stared regretfully at the potatoes, corn, glass ornaments, perfume, and other unfamiliar goods.

They had no idea when another caravan might arrive.

Vikir nodded toward the merchants instead.

“Look at *them*.”

Aiyen did.

The villagers were disappointed.

The merchants looked terrified.

“Their goods spoil.”

Most of the merchants' cargo was food.

Vegetables wilted.

Grain could rot.

Transport back through the jungle would destroy much of its value.

Ballak's side of the barter was different.

Medicinal ingredients.

Hides.

Spices.

Bones.

Gemstones.

Those could wait.

“If the market closes, Ballak loses very little. They do.”

One merchant snapped,

“Then we won't ever come back!”

“Fine. Other merchants will.”

Vikir already had another possibility in mind.

Once his body recovered and he returned to Baskerville territory, Cindywendy's commercial network could establish a much fairer route to Ballak.

The merchants whispered among themselves.

They still assumed there was room for profit.

Vikir offered them one chance.

“I know local market prices. Negotiate honestly and I may reopen trade.”

They scoffed.

“What would a kid know about prices?”

“Imperial trade is complicated.”

“We'll still profit. Taking a little less is fine.”

Vikir began naming numbers.

“Four gold.”

“What?”

“Four.”

“That can't—”

“Four.”

He knew what the merchants' goods cost locally.

He also knew what Ballak products sold for after reaching the Imperial capital.

That was hardly surprising.

*I was the one who reorganized Underdog City's commercial rules.*

As deputy magistrate, Vikir had handled diplomacy and trade directly or indirectly countless times.

He began appraising goods aloud.

Hellhound-tooth luxury pieces.

Monster-horn collectibles popular in the capital.

Naturally colored ivory.

Rare medicinal mushrooms.

He calculated acquisition price, wholesale value, retail value, distribution margin, labor, transportation, and taxes.

The merchants stopped smiling.

When Vikir finished, he moved only a small selection of Ballak goods onto their wagon.

A pair of antlers.

Some bear gall.

A few other high-value products.

“This is enough to cover what you brought and still leave a reasonable profit.”

The mountain of valuables had become a tiny pile.

The merchants' faces turned red.

They could not credibly dispute the valuation.

It was a fair transaction.

That was exactly why they hated it.

They had come expecting an absurd windfall.

Vikir was not done.

“Now we calculate damages.”

He took back even the small pile he had just set aside.

Then he held out his hand.

“Pay.”

The merchants stared.

“You're extorting us!”

“No. Punitive damages.”

“For what?”

“Selling defective food and distributing illegal drugs.”

Vikir pointed toward sacks of grain and vegetables.

Several contained seed.

He poured some into his palm.

“These were heated just enough to stop them germinating.”

Some merchants immediately looked away.

They had no intention of allowing Ballak to grow its own crops.

If Ballak became self-sufficient, the caravan could not return again and again to exchange worthless produce for priceless jungle goods.

Villagers began examining the seed in alarm.

Vikir picked up one of the glass beads the merchants had distributed as a gift.

He crushed it.

CRACK.

White powder spilled from inside.

Tiny holes in the glass slowly released the substance.

Kept beside a sleeper, it caused mild intoxication, drowsiness, and vivid dreams.

Long-term exposure damaged the body.

The same cheap narcotic had been incorporated into beads, brooches, necklaces, perfumes, and cosmetics.

Vikir's eyes narrowed.

*Did Morgue or Baskerville authorities knowingly tolerate this?*

Weakening Ballak's warriors through cheap narcotics—and leaving the next generation at risk of congenital deformities—would certainly benefit the Empire strategically.

Vikir did not care.

Right now, Ballak was sheltering him.

He was recovering here.

He was learning their archery.

Helping the tribe was more useful than helping Baskerville.

He smashed the drug-laced ornaments and separated out the heat-killed seed.

Sir Smuggler and the other merchants protested.

“Who are you to interfere? This is sacred trade!”

“The Forest God protects this market!”

“Ballak's shaman blesses our trade on the Forest God's behalf!”

Vikir answered calmly.

“The Forest God governs your stories. Baskerville's forest administration governs Imperial law.”

The merchants fell silent.

“If you want to keep arguing superstition, I can report you to Underdog City's Ministry of Justice.”

They recovered enough to bluster again.

“Our goods passed the Underdog food-and-drug inspection!”

“That approval was under the old rules. The current regulations require new review. Several of your products violate them.”

“What would a barbarian know about the new law?”

Vikir's gaze sharpened.

“You think I don't know the law?”

“Of course not! The new rules are strict and complicated.”

Sir Smuggler pointed at him.

“You've never even heard of **Vikir's Special Law**, have you?”

Vikir almost laughed.

The laws he had written were apparently still functioning.

He could not reveal himself here.

So he merely ended the conversation.

“This market is over. Be grateful you're leaving alive.”

Aiyen handled the rest.

Once she learned that the mysterious sickness afflicting villagers came from the merchants' narcotics rather than plague, heredity, or a curse, her anger became murderous.

Several Ballak warriors nearly started shooting immediately.

The caravan fled.

Vikir's reputation rose again.

A slave only days earlier.

Now one of the village's most discussed figures.

◆◆◆

After the merchants left, Vikir returned to his shelter.

THUD.

A smoked boar hind leg landed in front of him.

Vikir looked toward the thrower.

Ahun stood some distance away, face red, refusing to make eye contact.

“……Ahem.”

“What?”

“Thanks. For earlier.”

Before Vikir could reply, Ahun shouted over him.

“And heal already! I need you recovered so I can— so I can fight you properly!”

His ears reddened.

“Eat that and get better.”

Then he left.

The large piece of boar meat was from Ahun's own hunt.

Vikir stared after him.

*Did he eat the wrong mushroom?*

Some jungle mushrooms caused hallucinations.

If Ahun had eaten one, perhaps—

“Hey. Slave.”

Aiyen tapped Vikir's shoulder.

“I'm not a slave anymore.”

“You'll always be my slave.”

Vikir frowned.

Aiyen ignored the objection.

“Ahun changes whenever his little sister is involved. He's actually a decent older brother.”

“Sister?”

“The girl you helped today. **Aheul**.”

Vikir remembered her.

The young Ballak girl who had brought the sun dung beetle larvae to the merchants.

Aiyen continued.

“Aheul has always been physically weak. Some unexplained illness has been draining her for a long time.”

The cause now seemed obvious.

The drug-laced glass bead.

Without Vikir's intervention, the tribe might have continued buying the same products while Aheul and others grew steadily worse.

Aheul could eventually have been left severely debilitated. Continued exposure could also cause congenital harm in a later generation—or even kill her.

Stopping the trade had likely protected far more than one girl.

Aiyen ground her teeth.

“We should've killed those merchants when we had the chance.”

Before Vikir could respond, a warrior sprinted toward them.

“Order from the chieftain! All warriors assemble!”

Aiyen straightened.

“What happened?”

“The merchants we drove away are plotting something. It looks like they're preparing a night attack.”

Aiyen's expression changed.

For the first time since Vikir had come to Ballak—

she smiled brilliantly.
