“Why is *he* here?”

Vikir stared at the portrait.

Black hair.

Red eyes.

Sharp nose.

Heavy jaw.

No beard yet.

There was no mistaking Hugo.

Why would a portrait of young Hugo Le Baskerville be sitting inside a pendant in an abandoned Rokoko village?

Pomeranian crept closer and tugged weakly at the chain.

She wanted it back.

Vikir returned the pendant.

“Who are the people in the picture?”

His voice was too hard.

Pomeranian flinched.

Vikir tried again.

Same question.

Slightly softer voice.

The child still looked nervous.

Aiyen stepped in.

“You're interrogating her.”

“What did I do?”

“Smile.”

Vikir hesitated.

Then attempted it.

The expression was awkward enough that Aiyen went blank for a moment.

Vikir looked at her.

“It's working.”

Aiyen recovered.

“Slave. You *can* smile?”

His expression immediately returned to normal.

Pomeranian finally answered.

“Mama. Grandma. Grandpa.”

Vikir's thoughts accelerated.

An old pendant.

Young Hugo.

A blond woman.

A black-haired girl.

Mother.

Grandmother.

Grandfather.

*Then—*

The blond woman had to be Hugo's first wife.

**Lady Roxana.**

The girl between them was their first child.

**Penelope La Baskerville.**

And if Penelope had been taken by Rokoko and later given birth here—

then the child standing in front of Vikir was exactly who she claimed to be.

**Pomeranian La Baskerville.**

Hugo Le Baskerville's direct granddaughter.

Vikir remembered something John Barrymore had once told him.

Long before Vikir's birth, Hugo had not always been the same cold man.

He had once been affectionate.

Family-minded.

Vikir had dismissed the story as nonsense.

Now he reconsidered Baskerville history.

The family had once maintained its base much closer to the Imperial center.

Its move to the far western frontier near Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain had not been punishment.

Hugo himself had requested it.

His first wife, Roxana, died after childbirth.

Later, his beloved first daughter Penelope disappeared after being taken by people beyond the frontier.

After those losses, Hugo changed.

Hatred for the tribes beyond the Black Mountains deepened.

His interest in later wives and children withered.

He became the ruthless border lord Vikir knew—

a man who threw himself into monsters, war, and expansion.

The Emperor found that ruthlessness useful.

Baskerville became the Empire's iron-blooded sword on the frontier.

And now one discarded Baskerville hound had found a hidden branch of that bloodline inside Rokoko territory.

Vikir looked at Pomeranian.

*That makes me her uncle.*

The thought was strange.

Baskerville produced sons in abundance.

Daughters were extraordinarily rare.

Hugo's acknowledged direct household was almost entirely male.

Vikir wondered what Hugo would do if this tiny granddaughter appeared in front of him.

His first instinct was pessimistic.

Pomeranian also carried blood from the very people Hugo despised.

Vikir placed a hand on the girl's head.

She flinched but did not retreat.

“You weren't treated well here either, were you?”

Silence.

“Come with me.”

Pomeranian grabbed the post behind her and refused to move.

Vikir followed her gaze.

The stone grave.

Her mother.

Aiyen understood first.

She stepped forward, bowed to the grave, then spoke to the child.

“When people leave their bodies, they become stars.”

Pomeranian watched her.

“Your mother too.”

Aiyen pointed upward.

“What lies under those stones is only what she left behind. Her spirit is watching you from the sky.”

The child listened.

“She would want you to leave this terrible place.”

Pomeranian understood more Ballak than Vikir had realized.

Slowly, she released the post.

Then walked toward him.

She studied his black hair.

His red eyes.

“Mama. Hair. Eyes. Same.”

Vikir nodded.

“Baskervilles tend to look alike.”

He picked her up.

It was the first meeting between uncle and niece.

◆◆◆

Pomeranian came to Ballak village.

After leaving her mother's grave, she seemed to realize for the first time that she was completely alone.

She attached herself to Vikir.

“Uncle. Where?”

“Meeting.”

“Together.”

Vikir sighed.

He was not accustomed to a child clinging to his leg every time he moved.

But leaving her alone was not realistic.

He picked her up.

Then found a practical way to carry her.

The thick Oxbear pelt Akwilla had awarded him after the merchant incident became an improvised carrier.

*So this is what I'm using that expensive hide for.*

Pomeranian rode against his back.

Vikir headed toward Akwilla's tent.

“I'll have to ask Chihuahua for help later.”

Ballak village could not be the girl's permanent home.

Whether or not he ever brought her directly to Hugo, Pomeranian needed a place inside Imperial civilization where she could be protected.

Vikir was already planning a temporary trip outside the jungle.

He also wanted to see Morgue.

Two years of searches and a continuing Baskerville alliance were worth investigating personally.

He entered Akwilla's tent.

Akwilla.

Aiyen.

The elders.

All wore serious expressions.

Vikir and Aiyen had been summoned as direct witnesses.

Akwilla looked at them.

“So. A plague?”

They described Rokoko territory.

The dead hunters.

The abandoned village.

The red spots.

Other Ballak patrols had found similar corpses among orcs, lizardmen, and other humanoid peoples.

Akwilla's expression darkened.

“The rainy season is almost here.”

If disease was spreading already, heavy rain would make everything worse.

Ah'Heman stepped forward.

“Chieftain. This is not plague.”

He struck the ground with his staff.

“It is a curse.”

The shaman argued that ritual and sacrifice could solve it.

Several older elders agreed.

The moment was also convenient for anyone hoping to restore the shaman's weakened authority.

Younger warriors took the opposite position.

Disease.

Not curse.

Abandon the village.

Move before the rains.

The argument became a clash between tradition and survival.

Akwilla frowned.

“What do we do?”

Vikir raised a hand.

The words **Red Death** had come from him.

Now he spoke with certainty.

“I know how to stop this disease.”

The tent went silent.

And with that answer, the problem ceased to be only a mystery.

It became something Ballak could fight.
