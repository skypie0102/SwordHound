# Chapter 81: The Saintess (4)

> Chinese-primary reconstruction from `source/chinese/chapters/081.txt` — `b2ad1726acd434f02ec425e8736f672f4e07ca21`. Recovered English Chapter 80 is the verified shifted secondary witness. The chapter's supernatural healing system is treated as fictional mechanics, not real-world medical instruction. Dolores is sixteen; her growing admiration is kept nonsexual and source-level.

When Vikir first entered the severe-case ward, the priests tried to throw him out.

“This is no place for a layman.”

“You'll be infected.”

“We don't need help. You'll only interfere.”

That changed quickly.

Vikir stopped beside a priest treating a red lesion on a patient's leg.

“Wrong.”

The priest glared.

“That isn't the efficient way to use divine power.”

Ordinary Quovadis theology described miracles as a transaction of trust.

The priest prayed.

A god granted power.

The priest used that power in the mortal world.

Shared faith strengthened the connection.

Long devotion strengthened it further.

Prayer.

Service.

Sacrifice.

Charity.

Discipline.

A lifetime of religious acts determined how much divine power a priest could safely draw.

One unit of accumulated grace.

One unit of miracle.

That was common sense.

Vikir knew a later method that broke the equation.

*Short selling.*

In commerce, short selling meant selling something one did not currently own, then replacing it later.

During the Age of Destruction, priests had applied the same idea to divine power.

The wounded multiplied faster than faith reserves could recover.

Demons arrived faster than miracles could be performed.

At first, the gods merely raised the limits of priests with excellent records.

Even that was not enough.

So the priests learned to borrow from the future.

Perform the miracle first.

Repay the spiritual debt later through prayer, service, sacrifice, and accumulated merit.

Some called it divine borrowing.

Some called it shorting divine power.

Others called it an overdraft.

In that desperate era, even people who were not priests could sometimes draw a small amount of divine power if they had accumulated enormous merit fighting demons.

Vikir had killed more demons than he could count.

That merit remained attached to his soul even after regression.

So although he was no priest, he could draw a little divine power.

There was a condition.

He needed a sanctuary.

And Quovadis territory was one.

Vikir extended a hand.

A faint sacred light appeared.

The priests went silent.

A non-priest was using divine power.

That alone was shocking.

His efficiency was worse.

Better, from his perspective.

Impossible, from theirs.

Vikir had learned healing by watching priests in the worst years of the Age of Destruction.

He wasted nothing.

Rather than spreading power across intact skin, he directed it as close as possible to the afflicted tissue.

He used a scalpel where necessary.

Small incision.

Direct treatment.

Minimal waste.

Within the Quovadis sanctuary, he could sustain that limited power for short periods.

The priests crowded closer.

To them, this was revolutionary.

To Vikir, it was survival knowledge.

Then he surprised them again.

He mixed jungle herbs learned from Ballak.

The medicine did not cure Red Death.

It delayed symptoms.

Reduced fever.

Reduced pain.

Bought time.

Vikir moved from patient to patient.

Filth.

Curses.

Fear.

He accepted all of it without complaint.

His motives were not purely selfless.

First, he needed Quovadis priests confident enough to produce the holy water he wanted.

Second, he intended to take that holy water back to the people waiting in the jungle.

Third—

he felt responsible.

These Saint Mecca patients had become infected because of his own plan.

That guilt did not disappear merely because the plan had worked.

Then a child began screaming.

“Mom!”

A woman lay on the floor.

Only a few red spots had appeared.

Her infection was not yet severe.

Her body was.

Malnutrition had already weakened her dangerously.

Vikir placed a hand on the child's head.

“Don't worry. I'll treat her.”

He did.

Then he took a handful of gold from his pocket.

“Disease isn't the only problem.”

He gave it to the child.

“Buy food. Firewood. Take care of your mother.”

The child cried and bowed repeatedly.

Other patients saw the coins.

Hands reached out.

A priest panicked.

“You can't do that!”

Vikir looked at him.

“If you start giving away money, there is no end!”

“Of course there is.”

Vikir opened his pouch.

“The amount I have.”

He distributed the rest.

Every coin.

Then dropped the empty pouch and went back to treating patients.

The priests stared.

Dolores did too.

She remembered the young nobles who had followed her earlier.

They had brought wagons of goods.

They had announced their generosity loudly.

They had also looked down on the people receiving it.

Vikir's gift was smaller.

But he had emptied his own pocket without hesitation.

Scripture taught that sacrifice was measured by what giving cost the giver.

Dolores watched his back.

He worked in the dirtiest place in the ward.

He had the bearing of someone who had spent years on a battlefield.

A pilgrim.

An ascetic.

A prophet.

A powerful body carrying a wounded, lonely soul.

A man who called himself a hound while something inside him seemed lost and exhausted.

*Who are you?*

Again she remembered his words.

*Theology is fundamentally the process of understanding human beings.*

THUMP.

Dolores's heart jumped.

Once.

Then faster.

She was a saintess.

She was also sixteen.

And for the first time, her heart was racing toward a man whose face she had never even seen.
