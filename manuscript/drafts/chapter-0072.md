# Chapter 72: The Red Death (1)

> Chinese-primary reconstruction from `source/chinese/chapters/072.txt` — `8cf8c0385d0680afc3eb1148653abeac43ce12d9`. Recovered English Chapter 71 is the verified shifted secondary witness. Established terminology controls **Red Death**, **Ballak Tribe**, **Akwilla**, **Aiyen**, **Ah'Heman**, **Camus Morgue**, **Dolores Quovadis**, **Quovadis**, **Baskerville**, **Hugo Le Baskerville**, and **Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain**. The demographic material is retained clinically and without sexualization.

Vikir remembered the past.

The suspicious Imperials seen near the river source.

The red liquid.

The serpent-marked dagger.

And a disaster from his first life that had happened around this same time.

*The Red Death.*

A plague that covered its victims in red spots.

Vomiting.

Diarrhea.

Weakness.

A slow death.

It had swept through the jungle with terrifying speed.

Entire native communities had collapsed.

The scale had rivaled the great epidemics remembered by the Empire.

Eventually the disease reached Imperial territory.

In Vikir's previous life, Camus Morgue had raised a massive wall of flame at the frontier to stop its spread.

Dolores Quovadis had also been sent by the holy house of Quovadis.

Her divine power treated many of the infected.

But those measures protected the Empire first.

The peoples living deep inside Le Rouge et Le Noir Mountain had suffered catastrophic losses.

Nearly forty percent of the population in some regions had died.

That disaster had benefited Baskerville politically.

The mountain tribes occupied the upper levels of the local food chain.

When their numbers collapsed, lower-ranked monsters multiplied.

Monster waves became more frequent.

Civilian casualties increased.

And every new crisis made the border guardians more important.

Baskerville's influence rose.

So did Hugo's political power.

*I won't let that happen again.*

Vikir had lived among Ballak too long to treat their destruction as useful background damage.

◆◆◆

The dispute inside Ballak had not ended.

Older elders wanted ritual.

Younger warriors wanted evacuation.

Akwilla sat between both sides, brow furrowed.

She did not want to abandon the village.

Ballak had lived here for almost two centuries.

More than a hundred ancestral remains rested in sacred places around the settlement.

Ah'Heman understood exactly where to press.

“How can we abandon the land where our ancestors sleep?”

He struck his staff against the floor.

“We have lived here for nearly two hundred years. Who will protect their remains if we flee?”

Aiyen answered from the younger side.

“And what happens if every child dies?”

The tent quieted.

“What future are we protecting if the next generation disappears while we guard the bones of the dead?”

Ballak already had brutal child mortality.

Women began bearing children very young by Imperial standards.

Large families were common.

Even then, more than half of newborns died within the first month.

Only a small fraction of children survived beyond the first few years.

Malnutrition.

Disease.

War.

Hunting accidents.

Childbirth also killed mothers.

The Red Death on top of that could end entire bloodlines.

Akwilla finally raised her hand.

“What matters is how the disease spreads.”

The arguments stopped.

“What is the Red Death? How does it enter the body?”

No one had a reliable answer.

So everyone gave an answer anyway.

“A curse.”

“Something carried through the eyes.”

“The spirits of dead prey taking revenge.”

“Poison mushrooms.”

“Angry ancestors.”

“An insect with venom.”

“The Forest God has abandoned us.”

“Imperials brought it.”

Ballak people disliked saying *I don't know*.

Even a bad answer felt better than disappointing someone who had trusted you enough to ask.

Usually that trait was endearing.

Today it was useless.

Akwilla sighed.

Then Vikir raised one hand.

“I know how to stop the Red Death.”

◆◆◆

Ten days later.

A goblin hung upside down over a large pot.

Its wrists and ankles were bound.

Ballak warriors repeatedly lowered it into contaminated water, then pulled it back out.

The creature screamed and fought.

Eventually its movements slowed.

Red spots appeared.

Vomiting followed.

Then diarrhea.

The goblin stopped snarling and began shivering.

Ballak threw the infected creature onto an oil-soaked pyre.

Fire consumed it.

No one mourned a monster that preyed on human children.

Aiyen held the next rope.

“It's really the water?”

Vikir nodded.

“Contaminated water is the primary route.”

He pointed at another pot.

“Boil it first.”

Aiyen did.

Then she immediately lowered the next goblin into the boiling water.

The creature died almost instantly.

Vikir covered his face with one hand.

“Let it cool first.”

“……Right.”

Aiyen also covered her face.

They repeated the experiment properly.

The same contaminated water was boiled.

Allowed to cool completely.

Then used on another goblin.

Ten days passed.

The incubation period ended.

The goblin showed no Red Death symptoms.

Ballak erupted in relief.

“So boiling works!”

“The answer was water!”

“Vikir found it!”

People crowded around him.

Older warriors slapped his shoulders.

Younger ones stared with open admiration.

Vikir disliked being the center of celebrations.

Ballak did not care.

When the tribe praised someone, they did it thoroughly.

Vikir raised a hand.

“Boiling drinking water lowers the risk. But don't get contaminated water into your mouth or eyes.”

The noise faded.

“Stay away from wetlands where possible. Be careful with morning mist. Don't touch the waste or bodies of infected people with bare skin.”

He looked around the gathering.

“Prevention is easier than treatment.”

Aiyen stared at him.

“Is there anything you *don't* know?”

Vikir ignored the question.

Akwilla ordered hunting teams to spread the warning to nearby tribes.

The more people who knew, the fewer would die.

Then she approached Vikir personally.

The first time he had met Akwilla, her presence had felt like a mountain pressing down on him.

Now it felt different.

Warm.

Familiar.

Almost maternal.

“Because of you, we have a path out of this crisis.”

The Night Fox smiled.

“Thank you.”

Vikir bowed his head.

Ah'Heman interrupted.

“And when the rainy season comes?”

The shaman stared at Vikir.

“Tell them to avoid water when the entire jungle becomes water?”

It was a fair objection.

Rain would flood the rivers.

Wetlands would expand everywhere.

Moisture would saturate the air.

Aquatic monsters would crawl onto land.

Boiling drinking water would help—

but it would not solve everything.

Some Ballak warriors began murmuring.

Ah'Heman's confidence returned.

Vikir remained expressionless.

“Then we change the village before the rainy season arrives.”

Ah'Heman frowned.

“What?”

“Civil engineering.”

Vikir looked toward the low ground outside.

Drainage.

Flood control.

Raised living spaces.

If water was the danger, then the settlement itself would have to adapt.

And Vikir already had another reason to reshape the terrain.

This work would be useful for what came later.
